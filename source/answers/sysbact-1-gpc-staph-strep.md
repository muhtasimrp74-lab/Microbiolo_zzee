## 609
**Answer**

**Pathogenesis of a staphylococcal abscess**
1. **Entry and adhesion:** organisms enter through a hair follicle, a break in the skin or a wound, and adhere by **teichoic acid, clumping factor and fibronectin-binding protein**.
2. **Multiplication and acute inflammation:** peptidoglycan and toxins trigger release of cytokines, and **neutrophils** are drawn to the site.
3. **Tissue necrosis:** **α-haemolysin and leucocidin** kill neutrophils and tissue cells; dead cells, bacteria and exudate form **pus** at the centre.
4. **Localisation:** **coagulase** converts fibrinogen to **fibrin**, which is deposited around the lesion and forms a **fibrin wall**; **bound coagulase** also coats the cocci with fibrin and resists phagocytosis.
5. **Abscess formation:** the wall becomes a layer of fibroblasts and granulation tissue, giving a **thick-walled abscess** with a necrotic, purulent centre.
6. **Outcome:** the wall limits spread and also **keeps antibiotics and phagocytes out**, so the abscess tends to persist until it is drained; rupture may cause spread, bacteraemia and metastatic abscesses.

## 610
**Answer**

**Role of coagulase in a localized abscess**
1. *S. aureus* produces **free coagulase** (an extracellular enzyme) and **bound coagulase/clumping factor** on its surface.
2. **Free coagulase** reacts with a plasma factor (coagulase-reacting factor) to form a thrombin-like complex, which converts **fibrinogen into fibrin**.
3. The fibrin is deposited **around the growing colony and over the bacterial surface**, so the organisms are coated with host protein and are **not recognised or ingested by phagocytes**.
4. Fibrin also forms a **mesh at the periphery of the lesion**, which organises into the fibrous wall of the abscess.
5. The result is a **walled-off, localized lesion** rather than a spreading infection — in contrast to *Streptococcus pyogenes*, which is coagulase-negative and produces spreading lesions such as cellulitis.
6. The same wall **restricts the entry of antibiotics and leucocytes**, which is why surgical drainage is usually needed.

**Answer**

**Staphylococcal food poisoning**

- **Agent:** enterotoxin-producing strains of *S. aureus* (enterotoxins **A–E**, commonest A).
- **Source:** a food handler with a **septic lesion on the hand or nose**, contaminating the food.
- **Foods:** custard, cream, milk products, cooked meat, fish and salads kept at room temperature.

**Pathogenesis**
1. The organism multiplies in the food and **produces preformed enterotoxin** there.
2. The toxin is **heat-stable** (withstands boiling for 30 minutes), so **reheating the food does not destroy it**.
3. On ingestion, the toxin acts as a **superantigen**, binding MHC class II and the T-cell receptor to cause massive **cytokine release**.
4. It also stimulates the **vomiting centre through the vagus and sympathetic afferents from the gut**, which is why vomiting dominates.

**Clinical features:** short **incubation period of 2–6 hours**; acute vomiting, nausea, abdominal cramps, and little or mild watery diarrhoea; no fever; recovery within 24 hours.

**Diagnosis:** isolation of the same phage type from the food, the vomitus and the food handler's lesion; detection of enterotoxin in food by ELISA.

**Treatment:** supportive, with fluid and electrolyte replacement; antibiotics are not indicated.

## 612
**Answer**

**Why staphylococcal infection is difficult to treat**
1. **β-lactamase (penicillinase) production** by over 90% of strains destroys penicillin.
2. **MRSA**: the ***mecA* gene** produces **PBP2a**, with low affinity for all β-lactams, so every penicillin and cephalosporin fails.

4. **Abscess formation**: the fibrin wall and thick pus prevent antibiotics and phagocytes from reaching the organisms; pus is acidic and drug activity falls.

6. **Intracellular survival** inside phagocytes and epithelial cells, and the **small colony variant** phenotype, allow persistence and relapse.
7. **Carrier state** in the anterior nares leads to reinfection, and hospital spread is easy.

## 614
**Answer**

**Clinical importance of MRSA**

MRSA is *S. aureus* carrying the ***mecA* (or *mecC*) gene**, which encodes **PBP2a** and confers resistance to **all β-lactam antibiotics** — penicillins, cephalosporins and carbapenems.

1. **Treatment failure:** no β-lactam works, so the choice narrows to **vancomycin, teicoplanin, linezolid, daptomycin or clindamycin**.
2. **Multidrug resistance:** often resistant to macrolides, aminoglycosides and quinolones as well.

4. **CA-MRSA:** affects healthy young people, often **PVL-positive**, causing severe skin and soft tissue infection and necrotising pneumonia.
5. **Higher morbidity, mortality, hospital stay and cost.**
6. **Emergence of VISA and VRSA** from heavy vancomycin use.
7. **Control measures needed:** screening, contact isolation, hand hygiene, **mupirocin decolonisation** and antibiotic stewardship.

## 615
**Answer**

**Short note: MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*)**

- **Definition:** a strain of *S. aureus* resistant to methicillin and therefore to **all β-lactam antibiotics**.

- **Treatment:** vancomycin, teicoplanin, linezolid, daptomycin, or clindamycin by sensitivity.
- **Control:** hand hygiene, isolation, screening of patients and staff, nasal mupirocin, antibiotic policy.

## 622
**Answer**

**Importance of the ASO titre**

**Anti-streptolysin O** is the antibody formed against **streptolysin O** of *S. pyogenes*.

1. It gives **retrospective evidence of recent streptococcal infection**, when culture is already negative.
2. It is a **diagnostic criterion** (evidence of preceding infection) in **rheumatic fever** and **post-streptococcal acute glomerulonephritis**.
3. A titre **above 200 Todd units** in adults (above 333 in children), or a **fourfold rise** in paired sera, is significant.
4. The titre rises in **1–3 weeks**, peaks at 3–5 weeks, and falls over months.
5. **Limitations:** it is **not raised in skin infections (pyoderma)**, because skin lipids inactivate streptolysin O — anti-DNase B is used instead. It does not indicate the site of infection or prognosis, and it may be raised in healthy carriers.

**Also asked — clinical importance of streptokinase**
- Confirms recent streptococcal infection by the **anti-streptokinase (antifibrinolysin) titre**.
- Therapeutically, it is a **thrombolytic agent** in acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis, as it converts plasminogen to plasmin and dissolves clots.
- It also loosens fibrin in empyema and in infected wounds (with streptodornase).

## 623
**Answer**

**Pathogenesis of post-streptococcal acute glomerulonephritis (AGN)**
1. Follows infection with **nephritogenic strains of *S. pyogenes*** — **M types 12, 4, 25 and 49** (type 49 especially after skin infection).
2. Appears after a **latent period of 1–3 weeks** following pharyngitis, or 3–6 weeks after pyoderma.
3. It is a **type III (immune complex) hypersensitivity** reaction.
4. **Circulating antigen–antibody complexes** (streptococcal antigens such as **SPE B and nephritis-associated plasmin receptor** with IgG) are **deposited in the glomerular basement membrane**, forming subepithelial "humps".
5. The complexes **activate complement**, with consumption of C3 and release of **C3a and C5a**, which attract **neutrophils**.
6. Neutrophils release **lysosomal enzymes and free radicals**, damaging the basement membrane and causing **proliferative glomerulonephritis**.
7. The result is **haematuria (smoky urine), proteinuria, oliguria, oedema and hypertension**, with **low serum C3** and a raised ASO titre.
8. Prognosis is good in children, with most recovering fully; **antibiotics do not prevent it** once infection has occurred, and attacks do not recur.

## 624
**Answer**

**Pathogenesis of rheumatic fever**
1. Follows **untreated group A streptococcal pharyngitis** (never skin infection), with a **latent period of 2–3 weeks**.
2. Rheumatogenic **M types 1, 3, 5, 6, 18 and 24** are involved; genetic susceptibility is linked to **HLA-DR** types.
3. The mechanism is **molecular mimicry** — a **type II hypersensitivity** reaction.
4. Streptococcal antigens **share epitopes with human tissue**:
   - **M protein ↔ cardiac myosin, tropomyosin and sarcolemma**
   - **Group A carbohydrate (N-acetylglucosamine) ↔ heart valve glycoprotein**
   - Cell membrane antigens ↔ **neuronal tissue of the caudate nucleus** (chorea) and synovium
5. **Antibodies and cross-reactive CD4⁺ T cells** attack these host tissues.
6. Inflammation leads to the characteristic **Aschoff nodule** (perivascular granuloma with Anitschkow cells) in the myocardium.
7. The result is **pancarditis** (endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis), **migratory polyarthritis**, **Sydenham's chorea**, **subcutaneous nodules** and **erythema marginatum** — the Jones criteria.
8. **Repeated attacks** cause progressive valve scarring, giving **chronic rheumatic heart disease**, commonly **mitral stenosis**.
9. The organism is **not present in the lesions** — it is an immunological, not a septic, sequel.
10. **Prevention:** prompt penicillin treatment of sore throat (primary) and long-term **benzathine penicillin prophylaxis** (secondary).

**Answer**

**Post-streptococcal diseases**
1. **Acute rheumatic fever** and chronic rheumatic heart disease
2. **Acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**
3. **Erythema nodosum**
4. **Reactive arthritis** and **PANDAS**

**Pathogenesis of acute rheumatic fever**
1. Follows an untreated **group A streptococcal sore throat** by **2–3 weeks**; rheumatogenic **M types 1, 3, 5, 6, 18, 24**; host **HLA-DR** susceptibility.
2. It is a **type II hypersensitivity** based on **molecular mimicry**.
3. Streptococcal **M protein and group A carbohydrate share epitopes** with **cardiac myosin, sarcolemma, valve glycoprotein, synovium and caudate neurons**.
4. Cross-reacting **antibodies and CD4⁺ T cells** bind these host tissues and activate complement and macrophages.
5. **Aschoff nodules** form in the myocardium.
6. The result is **pancarditis, migratory polyarthritis, chorea, subcutaneous nodules and erythema marginatum**.
7. Repeated attacks scar the valves, most often causing **mitral stenosis**.

## 631
**Answer**

**a. Probable diagnosis:** **Acute rheumatic fever** following *Streptococcus pyogenes* (group A) pharyngitis — migratory polyarthritis of large joints, 3 weeks after a culture-proven sore throat.

**b. Mechanism**
1. Group A streptococcal throat infection with a **rheumatogenic M type**.
2. **Molecular mimicry (type II hypersensitivity)**: M protein and group A carbohydrate share epitopes with **cardiac myosin, valve glycoprotein, synovium and neuronal tissue**.
3. Cross-reactive **antibodies and T cells** attack these tissues after a latent period of 2–3 weeks.
4. Immune-mediated inflammation produces **Aschoff nodules**, carditis, **migratory polyarthritis** and chorea.

**c. Prevention**
- **Primary:** prompt diagnosis and **full 10-day penicillin** treatment of streptococcal sore throat (benzathine penicillin G, or erythromycin if allergic).
- **Secondary:** long-term **benzathine penicillin G 1.2 MU IM every 3–4 weeks** to prevent recurrence — for 5 years or to age 21 without carditis, and 10 years or longer with carditis.
- General: avoid overcrowding, improve living conditions, health education.

**d. Tests**
1. **Throat swab culture** for group A streptococci, and rapid antigen test.
2. **ASO titre** (and anti-DNase B) — raised or rising.
3. **Acute phase reactants:** ESR and CRP raised.
4. **ECG** (prolonged PR interval) and **echocardiography** for carditis.
5. Blood count, and application of the **revised Jones criteria**.

## 633
**Answer**

**Possible infection: infective (subacute bacterial) endocarditis**

1. **Background:** a previous attack of rheumatic fever leaves **damaged, scarred valves** with a roughened endothelium, on which **sterile platelet–fibrin vegetations** form.
2. **Source:** dental procedures without antiseptic precautions cause **transient bacteraemia** with the **viridans group streptococci** of the oral cavity — ***Streptococcus mutans, S. sanguis, S. mitis*** (also *Enterococcus* and HACEK organisms).
3. **Pathogenesis:** the organisms adhere to the vegetation using **dextran (glucan)** produced from dietary sucrose, multiply within the fibrin mesh where **phagocytes cannot reach them**, and the vegetation enlarges.
4. **Features:** low-grade fever, malaise, weight loss, **changing murmur**, splenomegaly, clubbing, anaemia, **Osler's nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots, splinter haemorrhages**, and microscopic haematuria.
5. **Complications:** valve destruction and heart failure, **embolic phenomena** (stroke, splenic and renal infarcts), mycotic aneurysm, and immune complex glomerulonephritis.
6. **Diagnosis:** **three sets of blood cultures** from different sites before antibiotics, echocardiography (vegetations), raised ESR and CRP — the **modified Duke criteria**.
7. **Treatment:** prolonged high-dose IV bactericidal therapy, e.g. **penicillin with gentamicin** for 4–6 weeks.
8. **Prevention:** **antibiotic prophylaxis** (amoxicillin 2 g orally one hour before the procedure) for at-risk patients, and good dental hygiene.
