## 817
**Answer**

**Allergy** is a **state of altered or exaggerated immunological reactivity of the host to a substance (allergen) that is harmless to most individuals**, resulting in tissue damage rather than protection.

- The term was coined by **von Pirquet (1906)**, from the Greek *allos ergos*, "altered reactivity".
- It usually refers to **type I (immediate, IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity** — asthma, hay fever, urticaria, food and drug allergy, anaphylaxis — though it is loosely applied to all hypersensitivity reactions.
- **Allergen:** the antigen that provokes it (pollen, house dust mite, animal dander, foods, drugs, insect venom).
- The response requires **prior sensitisation**; disease occurs on **re-exposure**.

## 818
**Answer**

**Atopy** is the **genetically determined (hereditary) predisposition of certain individuals to develop immediate (type I) hypersensitivity reactions against common environmental allergens**, through the production of **excessive specific IgE**.

- Occurs in about **10–20% of the population**; strongly **familial**, with linkage to genes on **chromosome 5q31 (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 cluster)** and to **HLA** type.
- Characterised by **high total and allergen-specific serum IgE**, a **Th2-biased** response with raised IL-4 and IL-5, and **eosinophilia**.
- **Clinical manifestations ("atopic triad"):** **allergic rhinitis (hay fever), bronchial asthma, atopic dermatitis (eczema)**; also urticaria and food allergy.
- Detected by **skin prick testing**, **RAST/specific IgE assay**, and a positive family history.
- **Distinction from anaphylaxis:** atopy is the **local, chronic, hereditary** form; **anaphylaxis is the acute, systemic, life-threatening** form and can occur in anyone.

- It requires **prior exposure (sensitisation)**; the damaging reaction occurs on **subsequent contact**.

**Anaphylaxis** is an **acute, severe, generalised (systemic) type I hypersensitivity reaction** occurring within minutes of exposure to an antigen in a previously sensitised individual, which is **potentially fatal**.

- **Mechanism:** the allergen **cross-links specific IgE bound to FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils throughout the body**, causing **massive, explosive degranulation** with release of **histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, PAF and tryptase**.

**Step by step**
1. **Sensitisation (first exposure):** the allergen is processed by an APC and presented to a **Th2 cell**, which secretes **IL-4 and IL-13**; these drive the B cell to **class-switch to IgE**.
2. **Arming of mast cells:** IgE binds by its **Fc portion to the high-affinity FcεRI receptors** on **mast cells and basophils** — the individual is now **sensitised**, though asymptomatic. IgE is **homocytotropic** and persists on the cell for weeks.
3. **Re-exposure:** on second contact, the allergen (which must be **at least bivalent**) **cross-links two adjacent IgE molecules**.
4. **Signal transduction:** cross-linking aggregates FcεRI, activating **tyrosine kinases (Lyn, Syk)**, raising **intracellular calcium** and causing **granule fusion with the plasma membrane**.
5. **Degranulation and mediator release**
   - **Preformed (primary):** **histamine** (the chief mediator), **tryptase, chymase, heparin**, eosinophil and neutrophil chemotactic factors
   - **Newly synthesised (secondary, from arachidonic acid):** **leukotrienes C4, D4, E4 (SRS-A — bronchoconstriction 1000× more potent than histamine)**, **prostaglandin D2**, **platelet activating factor (PAF)**
   - **Cytokines:** IL-4, IL-5 (eosinophil recruitment), TNF-α
6. **Early (immediate) phase — 5 to 30 minutes:** histamine-mediated **vasodilatation, increased vascular permeability (wheal and flare), bronchospasm, mucus secretion, itching**.
7. **Late phase — 6 to 12 hours:** **eosinophil, neutrophil and Th2 cell infiltration**, with major basic protein release causing **tissue damage and persistent inflammation** — responsible for the chronicity of asthma.
8. **Clinical outcome:** local (rhinitis, asthma, urticaria, eczema) or systemic (**anaphylaxis**).

**What happens in a sensitised atopic individual on exposure to the allergen**

1. The individual already has **allergen-specific IgE bound by its Fc portion to FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils** — from previous exposure. The cells are **"armed"** but quiescent.
2. On re-exposure the allergen **cross-links two adjacent IgE molecules** on the mast cell surface.
3. Cross-linking aggregates the receptors, activates **Lyn and Syk tyrosine kinases**, and causes an **influx of calcium**.
4. The granules fuse with the cell membrane and the cell **degranulates within seconds to minutes**, releasing:
   - **Preformed mediators** — **histamine**, tryptase, heparin, chemotactic factors
   - **Newly synthesised mediators** — **leukotrienes C4, D4, E4 (SRS-A)**, prostaglandin D2, PAF
   - **Cytokines** — IL-4, IL-5, TNF-α

   - **Skin:** urticaria, **wheal and flare**, angio-oedema

   - **Gut:** cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea

## 825
**Answer**

**Degranulation of mast cells in type I hypersensitivity**

**A. The sensitised mast cell**
- Mast cells (in tissues) and basophils (in blood) carry about **10⁵ high-affinity FcεRI receptors**.
- Allergen-specific **IgE binds by its Fc (Cε3) portion** to these receptors; the cell is thereby **sensitised**, and the IgE persists for weeks (homocytotropism).
- Granules contain preformed mediators in a crystalline matrix.

**B. The trigger**
- The allergen must be **at least bivalent** to **cross-link two adjacent IgE-FcεRI complexes**. Monovalent allergen or free IgE **cannot** trigger degranulation.
- Degranulation can also be provoked **non-immunologically** (anaphylactoid): **C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins**, morphine, codeine, vancomycin, radiocontrast media, physical stimuli (cold, pressure).

**C. Biochemical events**
1. Receptor aggregation activates **Lyn and Syk tyrosine kinases**, phosphorylating ITAMs.
2. **Phospholipase C** is activated → **IP₃ and DAG** → **release of calcium from intracellular stores and influx from outside**.
3. The rise in **intracellular Ca²⁺** (the key event) activates **protein kinase C** and causes **microtubule assembly**.
4. Granules migrate to the surface and their membranes **fuse with the plasma membrane (exocytosis)**, discharging contents into the extracellular space — the cell is **not destroyed** and can regranulate.
5. Simultaneously, **phospholipase A₂** liberates **arachidonic acid** from membrane phospholipids, which is metabolised by the **lipo-oxygenase pathway to leukotrienes** and the **cyclo-oxygenase pathway to prostaglandins**.

**D. Mediators released**
- **Preformed:** **histamine**, tryptase, chymase, heparin, ECF-A, NCF
- **Newly formed:** **leukotrienes C4, D4, E4 (SRS-A)**, PGD2, PAF
- **Cytokines:** IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, TNF-α

**E. Modulation**
- **Raised intracellular cAMP inhibits degranulation** — the basis of treatment with **β₂-agonists (salbutamol), adrenaline and theophylline**.
- **Sodium cromoglycate** stabilises the mast cell membrane by blocking calcium influx.
- **Corticosteroids** inhibit phospholipase A₂ and the late phase.

**Mechanism of cell damage in type II hypersensitivity**

Type II (**cytotoxic**) hypersensitivity occurs when **IgG or IgM antibody binds to an antigen on the surface of a cell or in tissue matrix**, leading to destruction of that cell by three mechanisms:

**1. Complement-mediated lysis**
- The antibody bound to the cell surface activates the **classical complement pathway**.
- The **membrane attack complex (C5b-9)** forms a transmembrane pore → **osmotic lysis**.
- *Examples:* **ABO-incompatible blood transfusion reaction**, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria.

**2. Opsonisation and phagocytosis**
- The cell is coated with **IgG (bound by FcγR)** and **C3b (bound by CR1)** on macrophages.
- The opsonised cell is **phagocytosed and destroyed**, mainly in the **spleen**.
- *Examples:* **haemolytic disease of the newborn**, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, drug-induced haemolysis and agranulocytosis.

**3. Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC)**
- **NK cells (CD16), macrophages, neutrophils and eosinophils** bind the **Fc portion of IgG** coating the target.
- Killing is by **perforin and granzymes** or lysosomal enzymes, **without phagocytosis and without complement**.
- *Examples:* graft rejection, tumour cell killing, antiparasitic eosinophil killing.

**4. Antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction (without destruction)**
- Antibody **blocks** a receptor — **myasthenia gravis** (anti-acetylcholine receptor), pernicious anaemia (anti-intrinsic factor)
- Antibody **stimulates** a receptor — **Graves' disease** (anti-TSH receptor) — classified by some as **type V**

**5. Tissue (basement membrane) damage**
- Antibody against basement membrane antigen recruits neutrophils and complement — **Goodpasture's syndrome** (anti-GBM, affecting lung and kidney)

**Pathogenesis of type II hypersensitivity**
1. **Antigen:** located **on a cell surface or in tissue matrix** — it may be
   - **Intrinsic (self)** — as in autoimmune disease (red cell antigens, acetylcholine receptor, glomerular basement membrane)
   - **Extrinsic** — a foreign antigen (transfused red cells, fetal Rh antigen), or a **drug or its metabolite adsorbed onto the cell surface** (penicillin on erythrocytes, quinidine on platteles, methyldopa altering the Rh antigen)

4. **Inflammation:** **C3a and C5a** released attract neutrophils, which release lysosomal enzymes and free radicals, damaging adjacent tissue (as in Goodpasture's syndrome).
5. **Cellular dysfunction without lysis:** antibody may **block** (myasthenia gravis) or **stimulate** (Graves' disease) a receptor.
6. **Time course:** minutes to hours after antigen contact.
7. **Examples:** incompatible transfusion, **haemolytic disease of the newborn**, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, ITP, agranulocytosis, Goodpasture's syndrome, rheumatic carditis, hyperacute graft rejection, myasthenia gravis, Graves' disease.
8. **Demonstration:** the **direct and indirect Coombs (antiglobulin) test**.

## 832
**Answer**

**Classification of hypersensitivity** — Coombs and Gell types I–IV (with type V added later); see Q819/821 for the full table with examples.

**Mechanism of type III (immune complex) hypersensitivity**

1. **Formation of immune complexes:** a **soluble antigen** (persisting in the circulation) combines with **IgG or IgM antibody** to form antigen–antibody complexes. The antigen may be
   - **Exogenous** — foreign serum, drugs, **persistent infection** (streptococci, hepatitis B, malaria)
   - **Endogenous** — self antigens, as in **SLE** (DNA–anti-DNA complexes)
2. **The critical factor is the size of the complex**, which depends on the **antigen–antibody ratio**:
   - **Large complexes** (formed in antibody excess) are **rapidly cleared** by the mononuclear phagocyte system — harmless
   - **Small complexes** remain soluble and are also cleared
   - **Intermediate-sized complexes, formed in slight ANTIGEN EXCESS, are the pathogenic ones** — they are too small to be cleared and too large to pass through, so they **circulate and deposit**
3. **Deposition:** complexes lodge in sites of **high hydrostatic pressure, turbulence and filtration** — the **glomerular basement membrane, synovium of joints, skin, choroid plexus and the walls of small blood vessels**. Deposition is favoured by **vasoactive amines** that increase vascular permeability.
4. **Complement activation:** the deposited complexes activate the **classical pathway**, generating
   - **C3a and C5a** — **anaphylatoxins** (increase permeability, release histamine from mast cells and platelets) and, critically, **C5a as a powerful chemotaxin**
5. **Neutrophil influx:** neutrophils are attracted to the deposits and attempt to phagocytose them, but, because the complexes are fixed to the tissue, they fail — **"frustrated phagocytosis"**.
6. **Tissue damage:** the neutrophils discharge their **lysosomal enzymes (proteases, collagenase, elastase), reactive oxygen species and inflammatory mediators** onto the tissue, causing **necrotising vasculitis, fibrinoid necrosis, haemorrhage and oedema**.
7. **Platelet aggregation** at the site causes **microthrombi**, local ischaemia and further release of vasoactive amines.
8. **Timing:** the reaction develops over **3 to 8 hours** (local, Arthus type) or **7 to 12 days** (systemic, serum sickness).
9. **Laboratory findings:** **serum complement (C3) is markedly reduced**, circulating immune complexes are detectable, and immunofluorescence of biopsy shows **granular, "lumpy-bumpy" deposits of immunoglobulin and C3** along the basement membrane (contrast the **linear** deposits of type II in Goodpasture's syndrome).
10. **Examples:** **Arthus reaction** (local), **serum sickness** (systemic), **post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**, **SLE**, rheumatoid arthritis, polyarteritis nodosa, farmer's lung and other hypersensitivity pneumonitides, and erythema nodosum leprosum.

## 833
**Answer**

**Mechanism of type IV (delayed, cell-mediated) hypersensitivity**

The only type **mediated by T lymphocytes and not by antibody**; it therefore **cannot be transferred by serum, only by cells (adoptive transfer)**, and takes **24–72 hours** to develop.

**A. Sensitisation phase (first exposure, 1–2 weeks)**
1. The antigen — typically an **intracellular organism (*M. tuberculosis*, *M. leprae*, *Leishmania*), a hapten bound to skin protein (nickel, chromate, urushiol of poison ivy), or a graft antigen** — is taken up by **Langerhans or dendritic cells**.
2. It is processed and presented on **MHC class II** to **naive CD4⁺ T cells** in the draining lymph node (and on MHC class I to CD8⁺ cells).
3. In the presence of **IL-12**, these differentiate into **Th1 effector and memory cells**, which circulate.

**B. Effector (elicitation) phase (re-exposure)**
4. On second contact, **memory Th1 cells recognise the antigen presented by local APCs and macrophages**.
5. They release cytokines:
   - **IFN-γ** — the key mediator: **activates macrophages**, increasing their MHC II, lysosomal enzymes, nitric oxide and killing power
   - **IL-2** — clonal proliferation of T cells
   - **TNF-α and TNF-β (lymphotoxin)** — endothelial activation, adhesion molecule expression, local tissue damage
   - **Chemokines (MCP-1), MIF (macrophage migration inhibition factor)** — recruit macrophages and **hold them at the site**
6. **Mononuclear cell infiltration:** over 24–72 hours, lymphocytes and especially **macrophages accumulate perivascularly**, producing **induration** — the hallmark of the reaction (in contrast to the oedematous wheal of type I).
7. **Tissue damage** results from the **activated macrophages' lysosomal enzymes, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species and TNF**, and from **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells** killing antigen-bearing cells directly.
8. **Granuloma formation (chronic form):** if the antigen **cannot be eliminated** (as with *M. tuberculosis*), the macrophages transform into **epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells**, surrounded by lymphocytes and fibroblasts, with **central caseous necrosis** — the **tubercle**.

**C. Variants**
- **Tuberculin (Mantoux) type** — peak at 48–72 h, induration; also the lepromin test
- **Contact dermatitis** — peak at 48–72 h, eczematous reaction; haptens such as nickel, chromates, cosmetics, poison ivy
- **Granulomatous type** — peak at 21–28 days; the most important clinically (TB, leprosy, sarcoidosis, schistosomiasis)
- **Jones–Mote (cutaneous basophil) hypersensitivity** — peak at 24 h, basophil-rich

**D. Clinical significance:** responsible for defence against **intracellular pathogens and tumours**, but also for the **tissue destruction of tuberculosis and leprosy**, contact dermatitis, **allograft rejection** and some autoimmune diseases (type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis).

**Definition:** hypersensitivity is an **exaggerated or inappropriate immune response to an antigen in a previously sensitised individual, resulting in tissue damage instead of protection**.

**Classification (Coombs and Gell)**
- **Type I — anaphylactic/immediate:** IgE and mast cells; minutes; anaphylaxis, asthma, hay fever

## 835
**Answer**

**Serum sickness**

- **Definition:** a **systemic type III (immune complex) hypersensitivity reaction** occurring **7–12 days after a single large dose of a foreign antigen**, classically **heterologous (horse) antiserum**.
- **Causes:** **anti-tetanus serum (ATS)**, anti-diphtheritic serum, anti-rabies and anti-snake venom sera, **penicillin, sulphonamides, phenytoin**, and infections such as **hepatitis B** and infective endocarditis.
- **Mechanism**
  1. The foreign protein **persists in the circulation** while antibody is being formed.
  2. At **7–12 days**, antibody appears and meets the still-abundant antigen, forming **intermediate-sized soluble complexes in slight antigen excess**.
  3. These **circulate and deposit** in the walls of small vessels, **glomeruli, joints, skin and heart**.
  4. They **activate complement**; **C3a and C5a** attract **neutrophils**, which undergo **frustrated phagocytosis** and release **lysosomal enzymes**, causing **vasculitis**.
- **Clinical features:** **fever, urticarial or morbilliform rash, arthralgia and arthritis, generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, albuminuria/glomerulonephritis**, occasionally myocarditis and neuritis.
- **Laboratory:** **serum C3 markedly reduced**, circulating immune complexes present, proteinuria.
- **Course:** **self-limiting** — resolves in days to weeks as the antigen is cleared.
- **Accelerated serum sickness** occurs in **1–3 days** in a previously sensitised person.
- **Treatment:** antihistamines, NSAIDs, corticosteroids in severe cases; **prevention** by using human immunoglobulin (TIG) rather than equine antiserum.

**Arthus reaction**

- **Definition:** a **localised type III hypersensitivity reaction** produced when antigen is injected **intradermally into an individual who already has a high level of circulating precipitating antibody**.
- **Described by Maurice Arthus (1903)** — a rabbit given repeated subcutaneous injections of horse serum develops an increasingly severe local reaction.
- **Mechanism**
  1. **Antibody is already present in excess** in the circulation.
  2. Injected antigen **diffuses into the vessel wall** and meets this antibody there, forming **immune complexes locally in and around the small vessels** (**antibody excess**).
  3. The complexes **activate complement**; **C5a** attracts **neutrophils**, and platelets aggregate.
  4. Neutrophil enzymes and free radicals cause **fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel wall, thrombosis, haemorrhage and local necrosis**.
- **Clinical features:** begins at **1–2 hours, peaks at 4–8 hours** — **erythema, oedema, induration, haemorrhage**, and in severe cases **necrosis and ulceration** at the injection site; resolves in 12–48 hours.
- **Clinical examples:** repeated **insulin, vaccine or antiserum injections**; **hypersensitivity pneumonitis** — **farmer's lung** (*Micropolyspora faeni*), **pigeon fancier's lung**, bagassosis — where inhaled antigen meets precipitating antibody in the alveolar walls.
- **Histology:** neutrophil infiltration with **fibrinoid necrosis**; immunofluorescence shows **immunoglobulin, complement and fibrin** in the vessel wall.

**The essential contrast:** serum sickness is **systemic, due to circulating complexes formed in antigen excess, appearing after 7–12 days**; the Arthus reaction is **local, due to complexes formed in situ in antibody excess, appearing within hours**.

**Transplantation** is the **transfer of cells, tissues or organs (the graft) from one site or individual (the donor) to another site or individual (the recipient or host)** with the aim of restoring lost or damaged function.

**Types of graft (by genetic relationship)**
1. **Autograft (autologous):** transferred from **one site to another in the same individual** — e.g. skin graft for burns, coronary artery bypass with saphenous vein. **Always accepted.**
2. **Isograft (syngeneic):** between **genetically identical individuals** — **monozygotic twins**, inbred strains. **Accepted.**
3. **Allograft (homograft, allogeneic):** between **genetically different individuals of the same species** — the commonest clinical situation (kidney, liver, heart from an unrelated donor). **Rejected unless immunosuppressed.**
4. **Xenograft (heterograft):** between **different species** — pig heart valve, baboon organs. **Violently rejected** (hyperacute).

**Other terms:** **orthotopic** (placed in the normal anatomical site, e.g. heart) vs **heterotopic** (placed elsewhere, e.g. kidney in the iliac fossa); **privileged sites** (cornea, brain, testis, anterior chamber) where grafts survive without matching.

**Answer**

**Measures to prevent graft rejection**

**A. Pre-transplant — improving donor–recipient matching**
1. **ABO blood group compatibility** — essential; incompatibility causes hyperacute rejection
2. **HLA (MHC) typing and matching** — particularly **HLA-A, B and DR**; **HLA-DR matching has the greatest influence** on renal graft survival; a sibling match gives the best outcome

5. **Mixed lymphocyte reaction** (historically, for class II compatibility)
6. Careful **donor selection**, minimising cold ischaemia time

**B. Immunosuppressive therapy** — the mainstay
1. **Induction therapy:** **anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG)**, anti-CD25 (**basiliximab**), anti-CD52 (alemtuzumab)
2. **Maintenance — triple therapy**

4. **Irradiation** of the graft, recipient or blood (total lymphoid irradiation); irradiation of blood products to prevent GVHD

7. **Use of immunologically privileged sites** (cornea, brain, anterior chamber) where matching is unnecessary
8. **Monitoring:** serial graft function, protocol biopsies, donor-specific antibody surveillance, and drug level monitoring

## 839
**Answer**

**Graft-versus-host (GVH) reaction**

**Definition:** a reaction in which **immunocompetent T lymphocytes present in the graft recognise and attack the tissues of an immunologically incompetent host** — that is, **the graft rejects the host**, the reverse of ordinary rejection.

**Billingham's three essential conditions**
1. The **graft must contain immunocompetent T cells**
2. The **host must be immunologically incompetent** (immunosuppressed, irradiated, immunodeficient, or a neonate) and so unable to reject the graft
3. The **host must express histocompatibility antigens foreign to the donor** (an HLA mismatch)

**Situations in which it occurs**
- **Allogeneic bone marrow or haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** — the commonest setting
- **Transfusion of non-irradiated blood** into an immunodeficient or immunosuppressed patient, or into a relative sharing a haplotype (transfusion-associated GVHD)
- **Transplantation of lymphoid-rich organs** — liver, small intestine, lung
- **Maternal–fetal transfer** in severe combined immunodeficiency; intrauterine transfusion

**Pathogenesis**
1. Host tissue damage from conditioning (irradiation/chemotherapy) releases inflammatory cytokines — the **"cytokine storm"** phase, with raised TNF-α and IL-1
2. **Donor CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells recognise host HLA antigens** presented by host APCs and are activated
3. They proliferate and differentiate into **Th1 effector cells**, releasing **IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α**
4. **Effector phase:** cytotoxic T cells, NK cells and activated macrophages attack **epithelial cells** of the target organs

**Clinical features — the three classic target organs**
- **Skin:** maculopapular rash, erythroderma, desquamation (may resemble toxic epidermal necrolysis)
- **Liver:** cholestatic **jaundice**, raised bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase
- **Gut:** **profuse secretory diarrhoea**, abdominal pain, bleeding, ileus
- Plus fever, weight loss, **severe immunosuppression with opportunistic infection** (the usual cause of death)

**Types:** **acute** (within 100 days) and **chronic** (after 100 days, resembling an autoimmune disease with scleroderma-like skin changes, sicca syndrome and bronchiolitis obliterans).

**Prevention and treatment**
- Careful **HLA matching** of donor and recipient
- **T cell depletion** of the graft (though this increases relapse and graft failure)
- **Immunosuppressive prophylaxis:** methotrexate with cyclosporine or tacrolimus, mycophenolate
- **Irradiation of all blood products** given to immunosuppressed patients
- Treatment: **high-dose corticosteroids**, ATG, monoclonal antibodies, extracorporeal photopheresis

**Beneficial aspect:** the **graft-versus-leukaemia (GVL) effect** — the same donor T cells also attack residual leukaemic cells, reducing relapse. The therapeutic aim is to retain GVL while limiting GVHD.

**Answer**

**Classification of graft rejection with immunological mechanisms**

**1. Hyperacute rejection**
- **Time:** **within minutes to hours** of restoring blood flow — on the operating table
- **Mechanism:** **preformed circulating antibodies (type II hypersensitivity)** in the recipient against donor **ABO or HLA class I antigens**, from previous **blood transfusion, pregnancy or transplantation**
- **Sequence:** antibody binds donor **endothelium** → **classical complement activation** → endothelial injury, neutrophil infiltration, platelet aggregation → **widespread thrombosis** → ischaemic necrosis
- **Outcome:** irreversible; the graft must be removed. **Prevented by ABO matching and a negative lymphocytotoxic crossmatch**

**2. Accelerated rejection**
- **Time:** **2–5 days**
- **Mechanism:** reactivation of **pre-sensitised memory T cells** with an antibody component (a secondary immune response)

**3. Acute rejection**
- **Time:** **days to weeks** — typically **10 days to 3 months**
- **Mechanism:** primarily **cell-mediated (type IV)** —
  - Recipient T cells recognise donor MHC by the **direct pathway** (intact donor MHC on passenger APCs) and the **indirect pathway** (donor peptides presented by recipient APCs)
  - **CD4⁺ Th1 cells** release IFN-γ and IL-2, activating macrophages and recruiting cells
  - **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells** kill graft parenchymal and endothelial cells by perforin/granzyme and Fas–FasL
  - An **acute humoral (antibody-mediated) component** causes necrotising vasculitis with **C4d deposition** in capillaries
- **Histology:** **dense mononuclear (lymphocyte) infiltration**, tubulitis and endothelialitis
- **Outcome:** usually **reversible with increased immunosuppression** (steroid pulse, ATG)

**4. Chronic rejection**
- **Time:** **months to years**
- **Mechanism:** **multifactorial** — repeated low-grade cellular and **antibody-mediated endothelial injury**, chronic cytokine and growth factor release (TGF-β, PDGF) causing smooth muscle proliferation; compounded by **non-immunological factors** (ischaemia–reperfusion injury, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, CMV infection)
- **Histology:** **progressive intimal thickening and fibrosis of graft arteries (graft arteriosclerosis)**, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, glomerulopathy
- **Outcome:** **irreversible and progressive** — the main cause of late graft loss

**5. Graft-versus-host disease** — the converse: donor T cells attack the immunocompromised host (see Q839).

**Short note: Tumour-associated antigens (TAA)**

- **Definition:** antigens that are **present on tumour cells but are also expressed on some normal cells**, usually at a different time (fetal life), in a different amount (over-expressed) or at a different site — they are therefore **not unique to the tumour**.
- **Contrast with tumour-specific antigens (TSA)**, which are **found only on tumour cells and not on any normal cell** (e.g. products of mutated genes and viral oncoproteins).

**Categories with examples**
1. **Oncofetal antigens** — normally expressed in fetal life, re-expressed by tumours
   - **Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)** — hepatocellular carcinoma, germ cell tumours
   - **Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)** — colorectal, pancreatic, gastric, breast carcinoma
2. **Over-expressed normal proteins** — **HER-2/neu** (breast), **PSA** (prostate), **CA-125** (ovary), **CA 19-9** (pancreas), tyrosinase and MAGE (melanoma)
3. **Differentiation antigens** — CD20 (B cell lymphoma), CD10 (CALLA in ALL)
4. **Viral antigens** — **EBV** (Burkitt's lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), **HPV E6/E7** (cervical carcinoma), **HBV/HCV** (hepatoma), HTLV-1 (adult T cell leukaemia) — strictly tumour-specific
5. **Altered glycoproteins and glycolipids**, and mutated oncogene products (**RAS, p53**)

**Clinical importance**
1. **Tumour markers** — aid in **diagnosis and screening** (AFP, CEA, PSA, CA-125)
2. **Monitoring treatment and detecting recurrence** — serial levels; a falling level indicates response, a rising one relapse
3. **Prognostication and staging**
4. **Targets for immunotherapy** — **monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab against HER-2, rituximab against CD20)**, **CAR-T cells**, and **cancer vaccines**
5. **Immunodiagnosis and tumour localisation** by radiolabelled antibodies
6. **Limitation:** because they are also present on normal tissue, they **lack specificity** — raised levels occur in benign conditions (CEA in smokers and inflammatory bowel disease, AFP in hepatitis and pregnancy, CA-125 in endometriosis) — so they are **not used alone for diagnosis**.

## 843
**Answer**

**Short note: Tumour immunity**

**Concept:** the immune system can **recognise and destroy tumour cells** because they express **new or altered antigens** — the basis of the **immune surveillance** hypothesis (Burnet and Thomas), which holds that transformed cells arise frequently but are eliminated before becoming clinically apparent.

**Tumour antigens**
- **Tumour-specific antigens (TSA)** — unique to tumour cells (mutated oncogenes, viral proteins)
- **Tumour-associated antigens (TAA)** — also present on normal cells (oncofetal antigens, over-expressed proteins)

**Effector mechanisms of antitumour immunity**
1. **Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8⁺)** — the most important: recognise tumour peptides on **MHC class I** and kill by **perforin/granzyme and Fas–FasL**
2. **Natural killer cells** — kill tumour cells that have **down-regulated MHC class I** ("missing self"), without prior sensitisation; also perform **ADCC**
3. **Macrophages** — activated by IFN-γ, kill through **TNF-α, nitric oxide and lysosomal enzymes**
4. **Antibody** — complement-mediated lysis and **ADCC**
5. **Cytokines** — IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-12

**Mechanisms of tumour escape (immune evasion)**
1. **Down-regulation or loss of MHC class I**, so CTLs cannot recognise them
2. **Absence of co-stimulatory molecules (B7)** → T cell anergy
3. **Antigenic modulation** — loss or internalisation of antigen under antibody pressure
4. **Secretion of immunosuppressive factors — TGF-β, IL-10, PGE₂, IDO**
5. **Expression of PD-L1**, engaging **PD-1** and inhibiting T cells; recruitment of **regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells**
6. **Antigen masking** by sialomucins; **blocking (enhancing) antibodies**
7. **Rapid growth outpacing** the immune response; **tumour-induced immunosuppression**

**Immunotherapy**
- **Checkpoint inhibitors** — anti-PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab)
- **Monoclonal antibodies** — rituximab (CD20), trastuzumab (HER-2)
- **Adoptive cell therapy** — **CAR-T cells**, tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes
- **Cytokines** — IL-2, interferon-α
- **Vaccines** — preventive (**HPV, hepatitis B**) and therapeutic (sipuleucel-T); **intravesical BCG** for bladder carcinoma

**Clinical evidence for tumour immunity:** spontaneous regression of some tumours (melanoma, neuroblastoma), lymphocytic infiltration correlating with better prognosis, and the **markedly increased incidence of malignancy in immunodeficient and immunosuppressed patients** (transplant recipients, AIDS).

**Immunological tolerance** is a state of **specific immunological unresponsiveness to a particular antigen**, in which the immune system fails to respond to that antigen while retaining full reactivity to all others.

- **Self-tolerance** — unresponsiveness to the body's own antigens — is essential; **its breakdown causes autoimmunity**.
- Distinguished from **immunosuppression**, which is **generalised and non-specific**.

**Tolerance of T lymphocytes**

**A. Central tolerance (in the thymus)**
1. Developing thymocytes with randomly rearranged receptors are tested against self antigens presented on thymic epithelial and dendritic cells.
2. **Positive selection** (cortex): thymocytes binding **self MHC with low affinity** survive — ensuring MHC restriction. Those that cannot bind die by neglect.
3. **Negative selection (clonal deletion)** (medulla): thymocytes binding **self peptide–MHC with high affinity** are **deleted by apoptosis** — the main mechanism of self-tolerance.
4. **The AIRE gene (autoimmune regulator)** allows medullary thymic epithelial cells to express **peripheral tissue antigens** (insulin, thyroglobulin) in the thymus, so that T cells against them are deleted. **Mutation of AIRE causes APECED/APS-1**, a multi-organ autoimmune syndrome.
5. Some self-reactive cells are instead diverted into **natural regulatory T cells**.

**B. Peripheral tolerance (outside the thymus)** — for self-reactive cells that escape
1. **Anergy** — a T cell receiving **signal 1 (TCR) without signal 2 (CD28–B7 co-stimulation)** becomes functionally unresponsive; engagement of **CTLA-4 and PD-1** reinforces this
2. **Clonal deletion by activation-induced cell death** — repeated stimulation triggers **Fas–FasL** apoptosis (defect → **ALPS**)
3. **Suppression by regulatory T cells (CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺)** — secrete **IL-10 and TGF-β** and compete for IL-2 (**FoxP3 mutation → IPEX syndrome**)
4. **Immunological ignorance** — antigens sequestered in **privileged sites** (lens, testis, brain, anterior chamber of the eye) are never encountered; their release after trauma can cause autoimmune disease (sympathetic ophthalmia)

**Factors favouring tolerance induction:** **immaturity of the immune system (neonatal/fetal — Medawar's experiments)**, **very high or very low antigen dose**, **soluble, simple, non-degradable antigens**, the **oral and intravenous routes**, antigen persistence, and concurrent immunosuppression.

**4. Neutralisation reactions** — toxin neutralisation (**ASO, Nagler's reaction, Schick test**), virus neutralisation

**5. Immunoassays using labelled reagents**

- **ELISA / EIA** — enzyme label
- **Radioimmunoassay (RIA)** — radioisotope label
- **Chemiluminescence immunoassay**, immunochromatographic tests

**Principles of two of them**

**A. Precipitation**
- When a **soluble antigen** reacts with its specific antibody in the presence of electrolytes at a suitable temperature and pH, an **insoluble lattice** forms and settles as a visible **precipitate**.
- Antibody must be **bivalent** and antigen **multivalent** for lattice formation.
- The reaction occurs best in the **zone of equivalence**; it is **inhibited in antigen excess (prozone of antigen)** and in **antibody excess (prozone phenomenon)** — hence sera must be titrated in dilutions.

**B. ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)**
- The antigen or antibody is **adsorbed (immobilised) onto a solid phase** (polystyrene microtitre well).
- The corresponding antibody or antigen in the test sample binds to it.
- An **enzyme-labelled (conjugated) antibody** — using **horseradish peroxidase or alkaline phosphatase** — is added and binds the complex.
- Unbound reagents are removed by **washing** at each step (the critical step).
- A **chromogenic substrate** (TMB, OPD) is added; the enzyme converts it into a **coloured product**.
- The **intensity of colour, measured as optical density in a spectrophotometer, is proportional to the amount of antibody or antigen present**.

**Principle of ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay)**

**Basic principle:** one reactant (antigen or antibody) is **immobilised on a solid phase**; the corresponding partner in the sample binds to it; an **enzyme-labelled conjugate** is then added, and the enzyme acts on a **chromogenic substrate** to produce a **colour whose intensity is proportional to the amount of analyte** present. Unbound material is removed by thorough **washing** at each stage.

**Components**
1. **Solid phase** — polystyrene microtitre plate wells, beads or membrane
2. **Enzyme label** — **horseradish peroxidase** or **alkaline phosphatase**
3. **Substrate** — TMB or OPD (for peroxidase), *p*-nitrophenyl phosphate (for alkaline phosphatase)
4. **Washing buffer** and a **stopping reagent** (sulphuric acid)
5. **Spectrophotometer (ELISA reader)** to measure optical density

**Types**
1. **Indirect ELISA — for ANTIBODY detection** (e.g. anti-HIV)
   - **Antigen is coated** on the well → patient's serum added; specific antibody binds → wash → **enzyme-labelled anti-human immunoglobulin** added → wash → substrate added → **colour develops if antibody was present**
2. **Sandwich (direct) ELISA — for ANTIGEN detection** (e.g. HBsAg, dengue NS1)
   - **Capture antibody** coated on the well → test sample added; antigen binds → wash → **enzyme-labelled detector antibody** added, "sandwiching" the antigen → substrate → colour
3. **Competitive ELISA** — the test antigen competes with a labelled antigen for limited antibody; **colour is inversely proportional** to the amount of antigen in the sample
4. **ELISPOT** — detects cytokine-secreting individual cells; **capture ELISA** and chemiluminescent variants

**Interpretation:** optical density is read at the appropriate wavelength (450 nm for TMB) and compared with a **cut-off value** derived from positive and negative controls; a standard curve allows quantitation.

**Advantages:** **highly sensitive and specific**, quantitative, suitable for **large-scale screening and automation**, safe (no radioactivity), reagents stable and relatively cheap.
**Limitations:** needs washing steps and an ELISA reader, is prone to **false positives from cross-reaction and non-specific binding**, requires a batch of samples for efficiency, and (for antibody tests) **cannot detect infection during the window period** — hence confirmation by **Western blot** or nucleic acid testing.

## 858
**Answer**

**"Antigen detection is superior to antibody detection" — justify**

1. **Direct evidence of the pathogen.** Detecting antigen demonstrates the **actual presence of the organism** in the specimen, whereas antibody only shows that the host has at some time **encountered** it — which may be past infection, vaccination or cross-reaction.
2. **Earlier diagnosis.** Antigen appears **as soon as the organism is present**, while antibody takes **1–2 weeks** to develop. Antigen testing therefore closes the **"window period"** — e.g. **HBsAg** appears weeks before anti-HBs; **dengue NS1** is positive from day 1, while IgM appears only after day 5; **HIV p24 antigen** shortens the window by about 1–2 weeks.
3. **Distinguishes current from past infection.** A positive antigen test indicates **active, ongoing infection**; antibody (especially IgG) persists for months or years after cure, so it cannot distinguish present from past disease. Treponemal antibody tests, for instance, stay positive for life.
4. **Valid in the immunocompromised.** Patients with **AIDS, malnutrition, malignancy or on immunosuppressants** — and **neonates** — may fail to mount a detectable antibody response, giving false-negative serology; antigen detection remains reliable.
5. **Useful in neonates.** Maternal **IgG crosses the placenta**, so antibody in a newborn may be maternal rather than the infant's own; antigen detection avoids this ambiguity.
6. **Monitoring treatment and disease activity.** Antigen levels **fall with successful treatment and rise with relapse** — e.g. **cryptococcal antigen**, HBsAg, HIV p24 — whereas antibody titres change slowly and unreliably.
7. **Rapid and point-of-care.** Many antigen tests are **immunochromatographic strips giving results in 15–30 minutes** at the bedside — malaria RDT, dengue NS1, rapid influenza and strep tests — valuable where culture is slow or unavailable.
8. **Works when the organism cannot be cultured** or when prior antibiotics have rendered culture negative — e.g. **latex agglutination for capsular antigen in CSF** after antibiotics have been started in meningitis.
9. **No cross-reactive anamnestic response**, which commonly confounds antibody tests (as in the Widal test in endemic areas).

**Qualification:** antigen detection is **generally less sensitive** than antibody detection when the organism load is low, is useful only during the **limited period when antigen is present**, and antibody testing retains value for **seroprevalence surveys, assessing immune status after vaccination, and late/convalescent diagnosis**. In practice the two are **complementary**, which is why combined assays (e.g. **fourth-generation HIV p24 antigen/antibody ELISA**) are now preferred.

**Mechanism of desensitisation (hyposensitisation / allergen immunotherapy)**

**Definition:** the deliberate administration of **gradually increasing doses of the allergen** to a sensitised person, in order to reduce or abolish the type I hypersensitivity response on natural exposure.

**A. Rapid (acute) desensitisation**
- Used when a sensitised patient **must** receive an essential agent — **antiserum (ATS), penicillin, insulin, chemotherapy**.
- **Very small doses are given at short intervals (every 15–30 minutes), each slightly larger**, over hours.
- **Mechanism:** each tiny dose **cross-links and consumes a small amount of cell-bound IgE, causing sub-clinical degranulation** that gradually **exhausts the mast cell mediator stores** without producing a systemic reaction. Mast cells are left temporarily unresponsive.
- The state lasts only while the drug is continuously present — it is **transient**, and sensitivity returns within days of stopping, so the full procedure must be repeated for the next course.

**B. Slow (chronic) desensitisation — specific allergen immunotherapy**
- Used for **allergic rhinitis, asthma, insect venom allergy**; allergen extract given **subcutaneously or sublingually** in increasing doses over **3–5 years**.
- **Mechanisms**
  1. **Induction of "blocking" IgG antibodies**, chiefly **IgG4** — these **intercept and bind the allergen in the circulation before it reaches cell-bound IgE**, preventing mast cell cross-linking (the principal mechanism)
  2. **Fall in specific IgE** after an initial transient rise, and reduced IgE production over time
  3. **Immune deviation from a Th2 to a Th1 pattern** — a shift from **IL-4 and IL-5** towards **IFN-γ**
  4. **Induction of allergen-specific regulatory T cells (Treg)** secreting **IL-10 and TGF-β**, which suppress both Th2 responses and IgE synthesis — now regarded as central
  5. **Reduced mast cell and basophil reactivity**, raising the threshold for degranulation
  6. **Decreased recruitment and activation of eosinophils**, blunting the late-phase reaction

**C. Precautions:** must be performed **under medical supervision with resuscitation facilities and adrenaline immediately available**, as anaphylaxis can be precipitated; contraindicated in severe uncontrolled asthma and in patients on β-blockers.

7. **Radioimmunoscintigraphy** — in-vivo localisation of tumours and infarcts with labelled antibodies

**B. Therapeutic uses**
8. **Cancer therapy** — **rituximab** (anti-CD20, lymphoma), **trastuzumab** (anti-HER-2, breast cancer), bevacizumab (anti-VEGF), cetuximab (anti-EGFR); **immune checkpoint inhibitors — nivolumab and pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1), ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4)**
9. **Transplantation** — **basiliximab (anti-CD25)**, muromonab (OKT3), anti-thymocyte globulin, to prevent and treat rejection
10. **Autoimmune and inflammatory disease** — **infliximab and adalimumab (anti-TNF-α)** in rheumatoid arthritis and Crohn's disease, tocilizumab (anti-IL-6R), omalizumab (anti-IgE) in severe asthma
11. **Infectious disease** — **palivizumab** against RSV in infants; monoclonals against **rabies, SARS-CoV-2, Ebola and anthrax toxin**
12. **Prevention of Rh haemolytic disease** — **anti-D immunoglobulin**
13. **Antidotes and detoxification** — digoxin-specific Fab fragments, anti-venom
14. **Targeted drug delivery ("magic bullets")** — antibody–drug conjugates and immunotoxins
15. **Cardiovascular** — abciximab (anti-GpIIb/IIIa) in angioplasty

**C. Research and industrial uses**
16. **Purification of antigens and hormones by immunoaffinity chromatography**
17. **Identification of cell surface markers** and elucidation of receptor function
18. **Standardisation of vaccines and biologicals**

**Advantages over polyclonal antisera:** **absolute specificity for a single epitope**, **unlimited and reproducible supply** of identical antibody, **high purity** and consistent batch quality.
**Limitations:** murine antibodies provoke **human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA)**, overcome by **chimeric (-ximab), humanised (-zumab) and fully human (-umab)** antibodies; they are expensive to produce; and their narrow specificity can fail if the target epitope mutates.
