## 578
**Answer**

**Ideal properties of an antimicrobial agent**
1. **Selective toxicity:** maximum toxicity to the microbe and minimum toxicity to the host.
2. **Bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic**, with a **broad spectrum** (or a suitably narrow one when targeted).
3. **Should not induce resistance** readily, and should be free of cross-resistance.
4. **Good pharmacokinetics:** well absorbed orally, with wide tissue penetration (including CSF, bone and the intracellular compartment), and a long half-life allowing infrequent dosing.
5. **Active in the presence of body fluids**, pus and tissue debris, and not bound excessively to plasma protein.
6. **Non-allergenic**, and free of serious adverse effects.
7. Should **not disturb the normal flora** or cause superinfection.
8. **Chemically stable**, with a long shelf life, and **cheap** and readily available.
9. **Should not interfere with host defences** or be antagonistic with other drugs; preferably synergistic.
10. Available in **oral and parenteral** forms.

**Classification of antimicrobial agents by mechanism of action**

**1. Inhibitors of cell-wall synthesis**

**Broad-spectrum antibiotic:** one active against a **wide range of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria** (and often chlamydia and rickettsia), e.g. **tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, ampicillin, third-generation cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, carbapenems**.

## 580
**Answer**

**Selective toxicity** is the ability of an antimicrobial agent to **injure or kill the invading microorganism without harming the cells of the host**. It is the basis of all antimicrobial chemotherapy (Paul Ehrlich's "magic bullet").

- It is achieved because the drug acts on a **target that is present in the microbe but absent (or sufficiently different) in human cells**.
- It is expressed as the **therapeutic index** = toxic dose for the host ÷ effective dose against the microbe. A high index means a safe drug.

**Examples**
| Target present only in the microbe | Drug |
|---|---|
| **Peptidoglycan cell wall** (absent in humans) | Penicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycin |
| **70S ribosome** (human ribosome is 80S) | Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol |
| **Folate synthesis** (humans take up preformed folate) | Sulphonamides, trimethoprim |
| **Bacterial DNA gyrase** | Fluoroquinolones |
| **Bacterial RNA polymerase** | Rifampicin |
| **Ergosterol** in the fungal membrane (humans have cholesterol) | Amphotericin B, azoles |

**Significance:** drugs with poor selectivity are toxic, e.g. **chloramphenicol** (bone marrow, because mitochondrial ribosomes are 70S), **aminoglycosides** (oto- and nephrotoxic), amphotericin B (binds cholesterol to some extent) and antiviral or anticancer drugs, where host and microbial processes overlap.

## 581
**Answer**

**How antibiotics exert selective toxicity** — see Q580.

They act on **structures or pathways unique to the bacterial cell**:
1. **Peptidoglycan cell wall**, which human cells lack: β-lactams and glycopeptides, so they are highly selective and safe.
2. **70S ribosome** (50S + 30S), differing from the human 80S ribosome: aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol. (Human mitochondrial ribosomes are 70S, which explains chloramphenicol's marrow toxicity.)
3. **Folate synthesis from PABA**, which humans cannot perform (they absorb preformed folate): sulphonamides and trimethoprim. Bacterial dihydrofolate reductase also binds trimethoprim 50,000 times more strongly than the human enzyme.
4. **Bacterial DNA gyrase and RNA polymerase**, structurally different from the human enzymes: quinolones and rifampicin.
5. **Membrane composition:** polymyxin binds bacterial LPS; antifungal azoles and amphotericin B act on **ergosterol**, which human membranes lack.

## 582
**Answer**

**Why antibacterial drugs do not act on viruses**
1. **Viruses are not cells.** They have **no cell wall, no cytoplasmic membrane, no ribosomes and no metabolic or enzymatic machinery of their own**, so the targets of antibacterial drugs simply do not exist.
2. **Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites** that use the **host cell's ribosomes (80S), enzymes and energy** for replication. A drug that blocked these processes would be equally toxic to the host cell, so there is **no selective toxicity**.
3. Specifically:
   - β-lactams and vancomycin need **peptidoglycan** — absent in viruses.
   - Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines and macrolides need the **70S ribosome** — viruses have none.
   - Sulphonamides and trimethoprim block **folate synthesis** — viruses do not synthesise folate.
4. Antiviral drugs therefore act on the **few virus-specific steps**: attachment and entry, uncoating (amantadine), **viral DNA/RNA polymerase** (acyclovir, which needs viral thymidine kinase), **reverse transcriptase** and **integrase and protease** (HIV), and neuraminidase (oseltamivir).

**Clinical point:** antibiotics are useless in viral illnesses such as the common cold, influenza and viral diarrhoea. Their misuse drives **antimicrobial resistance**.

## 583
**Answer**

**Mechanism of action of β-lactam drugs** (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams)

They are **bactericidal inhibitors of bacterial cell-wall (peptidoglycan) synthesis**.
1. The drug **diffuses through the cell wall** (through porins in Gram-negatives) to the periplasmic space.
2. The **β-lactam ring is a structural analogue of D-alanyl-D-alanine**, the terminal residues of the peptidoglycan pentapeptide.
3. It binds **covalently and irreversibly to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)**, which are the **transpeptidase, carboxypeptidase and endopeptidase** enzymes of the cell membrane.
4. This **blocks transpeptidation (the final cross-linking step)** of peptidoglycan, so the wall is weak and incomplete.
5. **Activation of autolysins** (murein hydrolases) then degrades the existing wall.
6. The weakened cell **swells and bursts by osmotic lysis**, because the internal osmotic pressure is high. Spheroplasts and protoplasts may form.

**Important points**
- They act only on **actively growing (log-phase) cells**, so they are ineffective against dormant organisms.
- They are **ineffective against *Mycoplasma***, which has no cell wall, and against intracellular organisms.
- **Resistance** arises from β-lactamase, altered PBPs (MRSA's PBP2a, pneumococcus), reduced porin permeability and efflux.

## 584
**Answer**

**Mechanism of action of penicillin** — penicillin is the prototype **β-lactam** and acts as described in **Q583**.

In brief:
1. Penicillin's **β-lactam ring mimics D-alanyl-D-alanine**.
2. It binds covalently to **penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases)** on the bacterial cell membrane.
3. **Cross-linking (transpeptidation) of peptidoglycan is blocked**, so a defective cell wall is formed.
4. **Autolysins are activated**, degrading the existing wall.
5. The cell undergoes **osmotic lysis**: penicillin is **bactericidal** against actively multiplying bacteria.

It is more active against **Gram-positive** organisms, because Gram-negatives have an outer membrane barrier and periplasmic β-lactamases.

## 585
**Answer**

**Mechanism of action of cephalosporins.** Cephalosporins are **β-lactam antibiotics** containing a 7-aminocephalosporanic acid nucleus, and they kill bacteria exactly as penicillins do (see Q583).

1. They penetrate the cell wall and bind **covalently to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)** in the cytoplasmic membrane.
2. This **inhibits the transpeptidase that cross-links peptidoglycan**, so cell-wall synthesis fails.
3. **Autolytic enzymes are activated** and the defective wall is degraded.
4. The cell lyses osmotically — a **bactericidal, cell-wall-active** effect, restricted to growing cells.

**Advantages over penicillin:** greater **stability to many β-lactamases**, a broader spectrum (progressively more Gram-negative cover from the first to the fourth generation), good CSF penetration with third-generation agents (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime) and anti-pseudomonal activity (ceftazidime, cefepime).

## 586
**Answer**

**Mechanism of action of sulphonamides and trimethoprim.** Both are **antimetabolites** that block the **bacterial folate synthesis pathway** at **two sequential steps**, a mechanism called **sequential blockade**.

```
   PABA + pteridine
        │  ← dihydropteroate synthase  ✗ SULPHONAMIDE
        ▼
   Dihydrofolic acid (DHF)
        │  ← dihydrofolate reductase   ✗ TRIMETHOPRIM
        ▼
   Tetrahydrofolic acid (THF)
        │
        ▼
   Purines, thymidine, methionine → DNA, RNA, protein
```

1. **Sulphonamides** are **structural analogues of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**. They **competitively inhibit dihydropteroate synthase**, so PABA cannot be incorporated into dihydrofolic acid. They are **bacteriostatic**.
2. **Trimethoprim** is a structural analogue of the pteridine portion of dihydrofolate. It **inhibits dihydrofolate reductase**, preventing conversion of DHF to **tetrahydrofolic acid (THF)**, the active coenzyme.
3. Lack of THF stops the synthesis of **purines, thymidylate and methionine**, so DNA, RNA and protein synthesis cease.

**Selective toxicity:** bacteria must **synthesise their own folate** and cannot take up preformed folate, whereas human cells absorb dietary folate. Moreover, trimethoprim binds **bacterial dihydrofolate reductase about 50,000 times more avidly** than the human enzyme.

**Note:** sulphonamides are antagonised by pus, tissue breakdown products and procaine, which contain PABA.

## 587
**Answer**

**Why sulphonamide and trimethoprim act better in combination (co-trimoxazole)**
1. **Sequential (double) blockade of the same pathway:** sulphonamide blocks **dihydropteroate synthase** and trimethoprim blocks **dihydrofolate reductase**, two consecutive steps in folate synthesis. Blocking a pathway at two points produces a far greater effect than blocking it at one.
2. **Synergism:** the combination is markedly more active than the sum of the two drugs alone. Individually each is **bacteriostatic**, but together they are **bactericidal**.
3. **Any dihydrofolate formed despite the sulphonamide block cannot be converted to the active tetrahydrofolate**, so the pathway is shut down completely.
4. **Reduced emergence of resistance:** an organism would have to mutate at **two independent targets simultaneously**, which is far less likely.
5. **Broader spectrum**, and useful against organisms resistant to either drug alone.
6. **Pharmacokinetic match:** both drugs have a similar half-life (about 10–11 h) and are given in the optimum ratio of **1 part trimethoprim to 5 parts sulphamethoxazole**, giving an ideal 1:20 ratio in tissue.

**Uses:** urinary tract infection, typhoid, **Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia**, nocardiosis, and respiratory infections.

## 591
**Answer**

**Why amphotericin B is selectively active against fungal cells and not human cells**
1. **It binds ergosterol.** Amphotericin B is a **polyene** that binds to **ergosterol**, the principal **sterol of the fungal cell membrane**.
2. Binding forms **pores (channels)** in the membrane, so potassium, magnesium, sugars and metabolites leak out. This causes **osmotic instability, loss of membrane integrity and cell death** (fungicidal).
3. **Human cell membranes contain cholesterol, not ergosterol.** Amphotericin B has a far **higher affinity for ergosterol than for cholesterol**, so it damages fungal membranes preferentially.
4. **Bacteria are unaffected** because their membranes contain no sterols at all (except *Mycoplasma*).

**However, the selectivity is only relative:** amphotericin B binds cholesterol to a limited extent, which explains its **dose-limiting toxicity** — **nephrotoxicity**, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, anaemia and infusion-related fever and chills ("shake and bake"). **Liposomal and lipid formulations** target the drug to fungal membranes and markedly reduce this toxicity.
