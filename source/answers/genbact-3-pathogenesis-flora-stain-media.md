## 528
**Answer**

**Toxic shock** (toxic shock syndrome, TSS) is an acute, life-threatening illness caused by **superantigen exotoxins**, chiefly **TSST-1** of *S. aureus* and the pyrogenic exotoxins of *S. pyogenes*. The superantigen cross-links the **MHC class II molecule to the Vβ region of the T-cell receptor** outside the antigen-binding groove, activating up to 20% of T cells and causing a **massive cytokine release (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ)**. It presents with high fever, hypotension, a diffuse erythematous rash with desquamation, and multi-organ involvement.

**Septic shock (endotoxic shock)** is shock caused by a systemic infection, classically by **Gram-negative endotoxin (lipid A)**. It is sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors and a raised lactate despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

**Biological effects of endotoxin (lipid A)**

Lipid A binds **LBP → CD14 → TLR4/MD-2** on macrophages, releasing **TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8**.
1. **Fever:** IL-1 and TNF-α are endogenous pyrogens acting on the hypothalamus through prostaglandin E2.
2. **Hypotension and shock:** **nitric oxide** and bradykinin cause vasodilatation, with increased capillary permeability and decreased peripheral resistance.
3. **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC):** activation of factor XII (Hageman factor) and tissue factor, with consumption of platelets and clotting factors, leading to thrombosis and bleeding.
4. **Complement activation** by the alternative pathway, releasing the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a (vasodilatation, chemotaxis).
5. **Leucocyte changes:** initial leucopenia followed by leucocytosis.
6. **Shwartzman reaction** (local and generalised), and **acute respiratory distress syndrome**.
7. **Metabolic:** hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, and multi-organ failure (renal, hepatic, cardiac).

## 538
**Answer**

**Endotoxic (septic) shock** is circulatory failure caused by **lipid A of lipopolysaccharide** released from Gram-negative bacteria, chiefly *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*, *N. meningitidis* and *Salmonella*.

**Pathophysiology**
1. **Release:** LPS is liberated on bacterial multiplication or lysis, including lysis after antibiotic therapy (the Jarisch–Herxheimer-like reaction).
2. **Recognition:** LPS binds **LPS-binding protein**, then **CD14** and the **TLR4/MD-2** complex on macrophages, monocytes and endothelium.
3. **Cytokine storm:** NF-κB activation releases **TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8 and PAF** — the central event.
4. **Vascular effects:**
   - **Inducible nitric oxide synthase → nitric oxide** causes profound **vasodilatation** and refractory **hypotension**.
   - Increased capillary permeability causes hypovolaemia, oedema and **ARDS**.
   - Myocardial depression lowers cardiac output.
5. **Coagulation:** tissue factor expression and factor XII activation produce **DIC**, with microthrombi plus bleeding.
6. **Complement activation** by the alternative pathway, releasing **C3a and C5a** (anaphylatoxins, chemotaxis, neutrophil activation).
7. **Consequences:** fever, tachycardia, warm then cold shock, lactic acidosis, acute kidney injury, hepatic failure, and **multi-organ dysfunction** with a high mortality.

**Note:** *N. meningitidis* endotoxaemia may cause adrenal haemorrhage, the **Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome**.

## 539
**Answer**

**Short note: Endotoxin**
- **Nature:** the **lipopolysaccharide (LPS)** of the outer membrane of **Gram-negative** bacteria. Its three parts are **lipid A** (the toxic moiety), the **core polysaccharide** and the **O-antigen**.
- **Release:** on cell lysis or death, including after antibiotic therapy. It is not actively secreted.
- **Properties:** **heat-stable** (121 °C for 1 h), poorly antigenic, **cannot be toxoided**, and is of relatively low potency.
- **Mechanism:** binds LBP → **CD14 → TLR4/MD-2** on macrophages, activating NF-κB and releasing **TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6**.
- **Effects:** fever, hypotension and **septic shock**, **DIC**, complement activation, leucopenia then leucocytosis, Shwartzman reaction, ARDS, and multi-organ failure.
- **Detection:** the **Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) test**, used to check intravenous fluids and devices for pyrogens. (Autoclaving does not destroy it; depyrogenation requires 250 °C dry heat.)
- **Examples:** LPS of *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *N. meningitidis*, *P. aeruginosa*.

## 544
**Answer**

**Principle of Gram staining** (Christian Gram, 1884). It is a **differential stain** that divides bacteria into Gram-positive (purple) and Gram-negative (pink), based on **differences in the cell wall**.

**Steps and principle**
1. **Primary stain — crystal violet (1 min):** all bacteria take it up.
2. **Mordant — Gram's iodine (1 min):** iodine forms a large, insoluble **crystal violet–iodine (CV-I) complex** inside the cell.
3. **Decolorisation — acetone or alcohol (10–30 s), the critical step:**
   - In **Gram-positive** bacteria the **thick, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan dehydrates and its pores shrink**, trapping the CV-I complex inside, so they remain purple.
   - In **Gram-negative** bacteria the alcohol **dissolves the lipid-rich outer membrane**, and the **thin peptidoglycan** cannot retain the complex, which is washed out.
4. **Counterstain — safranin or dilute carbol fuchsin (30 s):** the decolorised Gram-negative cells take it up and appear **pink or red**.

**Result:** Gram-positive = **purple**; Gram-negative = **pink**.

## 545
**Answer**

**Information obtained from a Gram-stained smear**
1. **Presence of bacteria**, and a rough estimate of their number (bacterial load).
2. **Gram reaction:** Gram-positive (purple) or Gram-negative (pink).
3. **Morphology:** cocci, bacilli, coccobacilli, vibrios, spirochaetes, branching filaments, and yeasts.
4. **Arrangement:** clusters (*Staphylococcus*), chains (*Streptococcus*), pairs with a lanceolate shape (*pneumococcus*), bean-shaped diplococci (*Neisseria*), Chinese letters (*Corynebacterium*).
5. **Special structures:** capsule (clear halo), **spores** (unstained refractile bodies with their shape and position), and metachromatic granules.
6. **Host cells:** **pus cells** (indicating inflammation), epithelial cells (indicating contamination of a sputum sample), and whether bacteria are **intracellular** (as in gonococcal urethritis).
7. **Provisional (presumptive) diagnosis** and the choice of **empirical therapy**, e.g. Gram-negative intracellular diplococci in urethral pus suggest gonorrhoea; Gram-positive lanceolate diplococci in CSF suggest pneumococcal meningitis.
8. Guides the **choice of culture media** and further tests, and assesses **specimen quality**.

## 546
**Answer**

**Principle of Ziehl–Neelsen (acid-fast) staining.** Certain bacteria, chiefly mycobacteria, have a cell wall rich in **mycolic acid, long-chain fatty acids and lipids**, which makes it waxy and impermeable to ordinary stains. Once such cells take up a stain, they **resist decolorisation by acid and alcohol**, a property called **acid-fastness**.

**Steps**
1. **Primary stain — strong carbol fuchsin**, with **heating** until it steams, for 5 minutes. Heat (and the phenol in the stain) softens the waxy coat and drives the dye into the cell, where it binds mycolic acid.
2. **Decolorisation — 20% sulphuric acid and 95% alcohol** (or 3% acid-alcohol): the lipid-rich wall re-hardens on cooling and **holds the dye**, so acid-fast bacilli stay red; all other organisms and tissue cells are decolorised.
3. **Counterstain — methylene blue** (or malachite green) for 1–2 min.

**Result:** **acid-fast bacilli appear bright red** against a blue background.

**Modifications:** weaker acid (0.5–1% H2SO4) for *M. leprae*, *Nocardia* and for *Cryptosporidium*, *Cyclospora* and *Isospora*.

**Answer**

**Classification of culture media by constituents**
1. **Simple (basal) media:** nutrient broth, nutrient agar, peptone water.
2. **Complex media:** contain extra ingredients for special purposes, e.g. blood agar.
3. **Synthetic (defined) media:** of exactly known chemical composition, used in research (e.g. Dubos medium).
4. **Special media:**
   - **Enriched:** basal medium **plus added nutrients** (blood, serum, egg) to grow fastidious organisms, e.g. **blood agar, chocolate agar, Loeffler's serum slope, LJ medium**.
   - **Enrichment (liquid):** favours the growth of the wanted organism while inhibiting others, e.g. **selenite F broth, tetrathionate broth, alkaline peptone water**.
   - **Selective (solid):** contains inhibitory substances that suppress unwanted organisms, e.g. **DCA, TCBS, LJ, MacConkey, Wilson–Blair, potassium tellurite agar**.
   - **Indicator (differential):** contains an indicator that changes colour, showing a biochemical property, e.g. **MacConkey** (lactose fermenters pink), **TCBS**, Wilson–Blair (black colonies).
   - **Transport media:** Stuart's, Amies, Cary–Blair, VR medium.
   - **Anaerobic media:** Robertson's cooked meat medium, thioglycollate broth.
   - **Sugar media** for biochemical tests.

**Selective vs enriched media**

| Selective medium | Enriched medium |
|---|---|
| Contains **inhibitory substances** (bile salts, dyes, antibiotics, high salt) | Contains **added nutrients** (blood, serum, egg, vitamins) |
| **Suppresses unwanted** organisms | **Supports fastidious** organisms |
| Used to isolate a pathogen from a mixed flora | Used to grow organisms that will not grow on basal media |
| Usually solid | Usually solid |
| *Examples:* DCA, TCBS, MacConkey, LJ | *Examples:* blood agar, chocolate agar, Loeffler's serum slope |

**Advantages of solid over liquid media**
1. **Isolated colonies** can be obtained, giving a **pure culture** from a mixed sample.
2. **Colony morphology** (size, shape, colour, haemolysis, pigment) aids identification.
3. **Bacterial count** is possible (colony counting, e.g. in urine culture).
4. **Mixed growth or contamination** is recognised at once; in broth it is not.
5. Used for **antimicrobial susceptibility testing** (Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion).
6. Allows **selective and indicator effects** to be seen, and easy subculture and preservation of strains.
