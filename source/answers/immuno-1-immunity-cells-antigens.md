## 760
**Answer**

**Immunity** is the **resistance of the host to infection by pathogenic microorganisms, their toxins and other foreign macromolecules**, together with the ability to recognise and eliminate altered self cells such as tumour cells.

**Classification of immunity**

**A. Innate (natural, non-specific) immunity** — present from birth, needs no prior exposure
1. **Species immunity** — e.g. humans do not get canine distemper
2. **Racial immunity** — e.g. sickle cell trait protects against falciparum malaria
3. **Individual immunity** — variation between individuals of the same race

**B. Acquired (adaptive, specific) immunity** — develops after exposure
1. **Active** — the host's own immune system responds
   - **Natural active:** after clinical or subclinical infection
   - **Artificial active:** after **vaccination** (live, killed, toxoid, subunit)
2. **Passive** — ready-made antibodies are transferred
   - **Natural passive:** maternal **IgG across the placenta** and **IgA in colostrum**
   - **Artificial passive:** **antisera, immunoglobulins (ATS, TIG, ARS)**
3. **Adoptive immunity** — transfer of immunocompetent lymphocytes
4. **Local immunity** — secretory IgA at mucosal surfaces

## 762
**Answer**

**Characteristic features of adaptive immunity**
1. **Specificity** — each response is directed against a particular **epitope**; the lymphocyte receptor distinguishes even closely related antigens.
2. **Diversity** — the repertoire is enormous (about 10⁹ specificities), generated by **somatic recombination of V, D and J gene segments**, junctional diversity and combinatorial pairing.
3. **Memory** — repeated exposure produces a **faster, stronger, longer-lasting secondary response** of higher affinity, mediated by memory T and B cells. This is the basis of vaccination.
4. **Self–non-self discrimination (tolerance)** — self antigens are not attacked, due to **central tolerance** (clonal deletion in thymus and bone marrow) and **peripheral tolerance** (anergy, regulatory T cells). Failure leads to **autoimmunity**.
5. **Clonal selection and expansion** — antigen selects the lymphocyte whose receptor fits and drives its proliferation into effector and memory clones.
6. **Specialisation** — the response is tailored to the pathogen: humoral against extracellular organisms, cell-mediated against intracellular ones; isotype switching adapts effector function.
7. **Self-limitation (contraction/homeostasis)** — the response declines when antigen is cleared, by apoptosis of effector cells.
8. **Affinity maturation** — somatic hypermutation in germinal centres yields antibodies of progressively higher affinity.
9. **Transferability** — can be transferred by serum (humoral) or by lymphocytes (adoptive).

**Premunition** (also called **infection immunity** or **concomitant immunity**) is a state of **immunity that persists only as long as the infecting organism remains alive in the host** — the resistance depends on the continuing presence of the pathogen, and is **lost once the organism is eliminated**.

- The host is **protected against reinfection (superinfection)** with the same organism, but is **not cured** of the existing infection.

- **Contrast with sterilising immunity**, in which the organism is completely eliminated and immunity persists afterwards (e.g. measles, mumps).

**Answer**

**Functions of macrophages** — as listed in Q771: phagocytosis and killing, cytokine secretion, antigen presentation on MHC II, effector function in CMI (IFN-γ activation), ADCC and opsonin-mediated clearance, scavenging, tissue repair and granuloma formation.

**Mechanism of phagocytosis**
1. **Chemotaxis** — the macrophage migrates along a gradient of **C5a, IL-8, LTB₄, bacterial N-formyl peptides** and chemokines towards the organism.
2. **Attachment (recognition)** — by:
   - **Pattern recognition receptors** (TLRs, mannose and scavenger receptors) binding **PAMPs** directly
   - **Opsonin receptors** — **FcγR** for the Fc of IgG and **CR1/CR3** for **C3b**; **opsonisation** increases the efficiency of uptake many-fold
3. **Ingestion** — the membrane flows around the particle as **pseudopodia**, which fuse to enclose it in a **phagosome** (requires actin polymerisation and energy).
4. **Phagolysosome formation** — lysosomes fuse with the phagosome, discharging their contents onto the organism.
5. **Killing**
   - **Oxygen-dependent:** the **respiratory burst** — membrane **NADPH oxidase** converts O₂ to **superoxide**, then **H₂O₂**, **hydroxyl radicals** and **singlet oxygen**; **myeloperoxidase** converts H₂O₂ and Cl⁻ to **hypochlorous acid** (the most potent). **Nitric oxide** from iNOS adds further killing.
   - **Oxygen-independent:** **lysozyme**, **defensins**, **cationic proteins**, **lactoferrin** (iron chelation), and **acid hydrolases** at the low phagosomal pH
6. **Digestion** by lysosomal enzymes, and **exocytosis of residual debris**.
7. **Antigen presentation** — surviving peptides are loaded onto **MHC class II** and displayed to **CD4⁺ T cells**.
- **Evasion:** some organisms subvert these steps — *M. tuberculosis* and *Legionella* inhibit **phagolysosome fusion**; *Listeria* and *Shigella* **escape into the cytoplasm**; *Staphylococcus* **catalase** destroys H₂O₂; cryptococcal capsule resists attachment.

## 775
**Answer**

**Killing mechanisms of the natural killer (NK) cell**

NK cells are **large granular lymphocytes (CD16⁺, CD56⁺, CD3⁻)** that kill **without prior sensitisation and without MHC restriction**.

**A. Target recognition — the "missing self" hypothesis**
- NK cells carry both **inhibitory receptors (KIRs, CD94/NKG2A)** that recognise **self MHC class I**, and **activating receptors (NKG2D, natural cytotoxicity receptors)** that recognise **stress-induced ligands (MICA, MICB)**.
- Normal cells display MHC I, so the **inhibitory signal dominates and the cell is spared**.
- **Virus-infected and tumour cells down-regulate MHC class I** (to escape cytotoxic T cells); the inhibitory signal is lost, the activating signal prevails, and **the NK cell kills** — the "missing self" principle.

**B. Mechanisms of killing**
1. **Perforin–granzyme pathway (the main mechanism)**
   - The NK cell forms a tight **immunological synapse** with the target
   - Granules release **perforin**, which polymerises in the target membrane in the presence of calcium to form **transmembrane pores**
   - **Granzymes (serine proteases)** enter through these pores and **activate caspases (and cleave Bid)**, triggering **apoptosis** with DNA fragmentation
2. **Fas–FasL pathway**
   - **FasL on the NK cell binds Fas (CD95)** on the target, recruiting FADD and activating **caspase-8 → apoptosis**
3. **Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC)**
   - **CD16 (FcγRIII)** on the NK cell binds the **Fc portion of IgG** already coating the target, directing lysis — the main **antibody-dependent** killing mechanism
4. **Cytokine secretion**
   - **IFN-γ** activates macrophages and promotes Th1 responses; **TNF-α** contributes to apoptosis

**C. Regulation:** NK activity is enhanced by **IL-2, IL-12, IL-15 and interferons α/β**.

**Significance:** NK cells provide **early defence (days 1–3)** against viruses before cytotoxic T cells develop, perform **tumour surveillance**, participate in **graft rejection**, and are deficient in **Chédiak–Higashi syndrome**.

## 776
**Answer**

**Recognition of foreign antigen by the CD4⁺ T lymphocyte**

1. **Antigen capture:** an **exogenous antigen** (bacteria, toxin, soluble protein) is taken up by a **professional APC — a dendritic cell, macrophage or B cell** — by phagocytosis, endocytosis or receptor-mediated uptake.
2. **Processing in the endosomal pathway:** the antigen enters the **endosome–lysosome** system, where **acid proteases (cathepsins)** degrade it into **peptides of 13–18 amino acids**.
3. **Loading onto MHC class II:** MHC II molecules, synthesised in the ER with the **invariant chain (Ii)** occupying the groove, travel to the endosomal compartment. The invariant chain is degraded to **CLIP**, which is exchanged for the antigenic peptide with the help of **HLA-DM**.
4. **Surface display:** the **peptide–MHC class II complex** is transported to the APC surface.
5. **Migration:** the dendritic cell matures and migrates to the **draining lymph node paracortex**, where naive T cells circulate.
6. **Recognition (Signal 1):** the **T cell receptor** engages the **composite surface of the peptide and the MHC II groove** — this **dual recognition is MHC restriction**. The **CD4 molecule binds the invariant β2 domain of MHC II**, stabilising the interaction and acting as a co-receptor; CD3 transduces the signal via ITAMs, **Lck and ZAP-70**.
7. **Adhesion:** LFA-1–ICAM-1 and CD2–LFA-3 interactions hold the cells together, forming the **immunological synapse**.
8. **Co-stimulation (Signal 2):** **CD28 on the T cell binds B7 (CD80/CD86)** on the APC, and **CD40L–CD40** interaction follows. **Without signal 2 the T cell becomes anergic or apoptotic** — a key mechanism of peripheral tolerance.
9. **Cytokine signal (Signal 3):** IL-12 → **Th1**; IL-4 → **Th2**; IL-6 + TGF-β → **Th17**; TGF-β alone → **Treg**.
10. **Activation:** rising intracellular calcium activates **NFAT, NF-κB and AP-1**; the cell secretes **IL-2**, expresses **CD25**, and undergoes **clonal expansion** into effector and memory cells.

**Key point:** CD4⁺ T cells **cannot recognise free or native antigen** — antigen must be **processed and presented on MHC class II** by another cell.

## 777
**Answer**

**"The immune response is not always beneficial to the host" — explain**

Although the immune response normally protects, the same mechanisms can injure the host:

1. **Hypersensitivity reactions** — an exaggerated or inappropriate response to a harmless antigen
   - **Type I:** anaphylaxis, asthma, hay fever, food allergy — IgE and mast cells
   - **Type II:** haemolytic disease of the newborn, incompatible transfusion reaction, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
   - **Type III:** serum sickness, Arthus reaction, **post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**, SLE
   - **Type IV:** contact dermatitis, the **tuberculin reaction**, and the tissue destruction of **tuberculosis and leprosy** — in TB, the caseation and cavitation are caused by delayed hypersensitivity, not by bacterial toxins
2. **Autoimmunity** — loss of self-tolerance leads to attack on self tissues: rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, type 1 diabetes, Graves' disease, myasthenia gravis
3. **Immunopathology in infection** — much of the damage in **rheumatic fever** (molecular mimicry), **dengue haemorrhagic fever** (antibody-dependent enhancement), leprosy reactions (ENL) and hepatitis B (CTL-mediated hepatocyte death) is caused by the immune response itself
4. **Transplant rejection** — the response destroys a life-saving graft; and in **graft-versus-host disease** the grafted lymphocytes attack the host
5. **Haemolytic disease of the newborn** — maternal anti-D IgG crosses the placenta and destroys fetal red cells
6. **Immunosuppression caused by the response** — e.g. measles-induced anergy
7. **Immune evasion exploited by pathogens** — antigenic variation, and **enhancing antibodies**
8. **Tumour immune escape** and chronic inflammation predisposing to malignancy
9. **Excessive cytokine release** — the **cytokine storm** of sepsis and severe viral infection, causing shock and ARDS

**Conclusion:** immunity is a double-edged sword — protective when regulated, damaging when excessive, misdirected or misregulated.

3. **Class of antibody:** **IgM predominates** in the primary; **IgG predominates** in the secondary, after class switching.
4. **Affinity:** low in the primary; **high**, from affinity maturation, in the secondary.
5. **Duration:** short-lived in the primary; **long-lasting** in the secondary.
6. **Cells involved:** naive lymphocytes in the primary; **memory cells** in the secondary.
7. **Antigen dose required:** large, often with adjuvant, in the primary; **small** in the secondary.
8. **Practical application:** the secondary response is the basis of the **booster dose** in vaccination.

## 780
**Answer**

**Immunogen** is a substance that, when introduced into the body, is **capable of inducing a specific immune response — the production of antibodies and/or sensitised T lymphocytes — and of reacting specifically with the products of that response**.

- **Immunogenicity** is the ability to **induce** a response; **antigenicity** is the ability to **react** with the products of a response.
- **Every immunogen is an antigen, but every antigen is not an immunogen** — a **hapten** is antigenic (it binds antibody) but **not immunogenic** on its own.
- Good immunogens are **foreign, large (>10 kDa), chemically complex and degradable** — e.g. proteins and polysaccharides.

## 781
**Answer**

**Antigen** is **any substance which, when introduced into the body, is capable of reacting specifically with the products of the immune response (antibodies or sensitised T cells)**, and which in most cases also induces that response.

- **Classically:** a substance that **induces an immune response and reacts specifically with it**.
- **Modern usage:** "antigen" emphasises the **reactivity**, and **"immunogen"** the **inducing capacity** — so a hapten is an antigen but not an immunogen.
- **Epitope (antigenic determinant):** the small part of the antigen that actually binds the antibody or TCR; **valency** is the number of epitopes per molecule.
- **Types:** complete antigen vs hapten; T-dependent vs T-independent; exogenous, endogenous and autoantigen; heterophile antigen; superantigen.

## 782
**Answer**

**Criteria of a good antigen (immunogen)**

1. **Foreignness** — the single most important criterion. The substance must be recognised as non-self; the greater the phylogenetic distance from the host, the better the response. Self antigens are tolerated.
2. **Large molecular size** — generally **above 14 kDa**; the best immunogens exceed 100 kDa (e.g. haemocyanin, tetanus toxoid). Substances below **5 kDa** are poor or non-immunogenic.
3. **Chemical complexity** — a **heterogeneous structure with many different amino acids, particularly aromatic ones (tyrosine)**, is required. A large but repetitive homopolymer (e.g. nylon, gelatin) is a poor immunogen.
4. **Chemical nature** — **proteins are the best immunogens**, followed by **polysaccharides**; lipids and nucleic acids are poorly immunogenic alone, becoming immunogenic when conjugated to a protein carrier.
5. **Susceptibility to tissue enzymes (degradability)** — the antigen must be capable of being **processed and presented** by APCs; non-degradable substances such as plastics and metals are not immunogenic.
6. **Stable three-dimensional structure (conformation)** and adequate **rigidity**.
7. **Physical form** — **particulate and denatured** antigens are more immunogenic than soluble, native ones.
8. **Dosage, route and timing** — an **optimum dose** is needed (too little or too much induces tolerance); **subcutaneous and intradermal** routes are more immunogenic than intravenous or oral; repeated or spaced doses enhance the response.
9. **Use of an adjuvant** — alum, Freund's adjuvant or MF59 greatly enhances and prolongs immunogenicity.
10. **Host factors** — the **genetic constitution (MHC/HLA type)** of the host, its species, age, nutritional and immune status determine responsiveness.

## 783
**Answer**

**Criteria/properties of a good antigen** — in brief:
1. **Foreignness** — must be non-self (most important)
2. **High molecular weight** — generally **>14 kDa**; best above 100 kDa
3. **Chemical complexity** — varied structure with aromatic amino acids; repeating homopolymers are poor
4. **Chemical nature** — **proteins > polysaccharides >> lipids and nucleic acids**
5. **Degradability** — must be processed and presented by APCs
6. **Structural stability and rigidity**
7. **Physical form** — particulate and denatured forms are more immunogenic
8. **Optimal dose and suitable route** (subcutaneous/intradermal), with repeat exposure
9. **Adjuvant** enhances the response
10. **Host genetic (HLA) makeup**, age and immune status

## 784
**Answer**

**Hapten**

A **hapten** is a **low molecular weight substance that is not immunogenic by itself, but becomes immunogenic when conjugated to a large carrier molecule (usually a protein)**; once antibodies have been formed, the hapten **can react with them on its own**.

- Haptens are therefore **antigenic but not immunogenic** — they have **antigenicity without immunogenicity**, and are usually **univalent**.
- **Types:** **complex haptens** (precipitate with antibody) and **simple haptens** (combine but do not precipitate, and can block the reaction).
- **Examples:** **penicillin and its degradation products** (penicilloyl group binds serum proteins → penicillin allergy), **urushiol of poison ivy** (contact dermatitis), aspirin, sulphonamides, quinidine, **polysaccharide of the pneumococcal capsule** (in some contexts), picryl chloride, nickel salts.
- **Clinical importance:** the basis of most **drug allergies** and of **contact hypersensitivity**; also used experimentally to study antibody specificity (Landsteiner's work).

**Epitope (antigenic determinant)** is the **smallest part of an antigen that is recognised by, and binds to, the antigen-binding site of an antibody or the T cell receptor**.
- Usually comprises **5–8 amino acids or sugar residues**.
- **Linear (sequential)** epitopes are formed by a continuous amino acid sequence; **conformational** epitopes are formed by residues brought together by folding.
- The **number of epitopes per antigen molecule is its valency**.
- B cells recognise epitopes on the **native** molecule; T cells recognise **processed linear peptides presented on MHC**.

## 785
**Answer**

**Antigen (complete antigen) vs hapten**

| Feature | **Complete antigen** | **Hapten (incomplete antigen)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Immunogenicity** | **Present** — induces an immune response alone | **Absent** — cannot induce a response alone |
| **Antigenicity (reactivity)** | **Present** — reacts with antibody | **Present** — reacts with antibody once formed |
| **Molecular weight** | **High** (usually >14 kDa) | **Low** (usually <5 kDa) |
| Chemical nature | Proteins, polysaccharides | Simple chemicals, drugs, lipids |
| **Carrier required** | **Not required** | **Required** — must conjugate to a protein carrier |
| **Valency** | **Multivalent** (many epitopes) | Usually **univalent** |
| Precipitation with antibody | Occurs | Does not occur (simple haptens) |
| Degradability/processing | Processed and presented | Needs the carrier for processing |
| Examples | Tetanus toxoid, bacterial proteins, serum albumin | **Penicillin**, urushiol (poison ivy), aspirin, sulphonamides, nickel |

**Key concept:** the **hapten supplies the epitope** and determines the specificity, while the **carrier supplies the T cell epitopes** needed for help — the "hapten–carrier effect".

## 786
**Answer**

**T-dependent vs T-independent antigens**

| Feature | **T-dependent antigen** | **T-independent antigen** |
|---|---|---|
| **Need for T cell help** | **Required** — B cell needs help from CD4⁺ Th cells | **Not required** — directly activates B cells |
| **Chemical nature** | **Proteins** and glycoproteins | **Polysaccharides**, lipopolysaccharide, polymeric flagellin |
| Structure | Complex, varied epitopes | **Repeating identical epitopes** that cross-link the BCR |
| **Antibody class** | **All classes** — class switching to IgG, IgA, IgE | **Mainly IgM**; little or no switching |
| **Memory** | **Present** | **Absent or poor** |
| **Affinity maturation** | **Present** | **Absent** |
| Response in infants (<2 years) | Good | **Poor** — infants respond badly to polysaccharides |
| Response in athymic (nude) mice | **Absent** | **Present** |
| Examples | Tetanus and diphtheria toxoid, serum proteins, viral proteins | **Pneumococcal and meningococcal capsular polysaccharide**, Hib polysaccharide, LPS, dextran, Ficoll |

**Clinical application:** because plain polysaccharide vaccines are T-independent and fail to protect infants, they are **conjugated to a protein carrier** (tetanus toxoid or CRM197) — the **conjugate vaccines (Hib, pneumococcal, meningococcal)** — converting them into **T-dependent antigens** that induce **IgG and lasting memory** even in young children.

## 787
**Answer**

**Superantigens** are a class of **microbial proteins that activate a very large proportion of T lymphocytes (up to 20% of the total, compared with 0.01% for a conventional antigen) in a non-specific manner**.

**Mechanism**
- Unlike a conventional antigen, a superantigen is **not processed**. It binds **simultaneously to the outside of the MHC class II molecule on the APC and to the Vβ region of the T cell receptor**, **outside the antigen-binding groove**.
- This **cross-links APC and T cell irrespective of antigen specificity**, activating all T cells bearing that particular Vβ family.
- The result is **massive, polyclonal T cell activation** with a **cytokine storm — IL-1, IL-2, IFN-γ and especially TNF-α** — causing fever, capillary leak, hypotension, shock and multi-organ failure; later, **clonal deletion or anergy** of the affected T cells, leaving immunosuppression.

**Two examples**
1. **Toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1)** of ***Staphylococcus aureus*** — toxic shock syndrome
2. **Streptococcal pyrogenic (erythrogenic) exotoxins A and C** of ***Streptococcus pyogenes*** — scarlet fever and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome

*(Others: staphylococcal enterotoxins A–E, exfoliative toxin, *Mycoplasma arthritidis* mitogen, and the retroviral MMTV antigens.)*
