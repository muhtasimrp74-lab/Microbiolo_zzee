## 692
**Answer**

**Pathogenesis of *Salmonella* Typhi infection**
1. **Infecting dose** of about **10⁵–10⁸ organisms** is ingested in contaminated food or water (faeco-oral, the "5 Fs"); gastric acid is an important barrier, so achlorhydria and antacids lower the dose needed.
2. Surviving bacilli reach the **small intestine**, attach to and **invade the M cells over the Peyer's patches** using a **type III secretion system** (*Salmonella* pathogenicity island 1).
3. They are taken up by **macrophages but resist intracellular killing** (Vi antigen and the PhoP/PhoQ system), and **multiply within them**.
4. They pass to the **mesenteric lymph nodes**, and through the thoracic duct reach the blood — the **primary (transient) bacteraemia**, which is asymptomatic and corresponds to the **incubation period of 7–14 days**.
5. Organisms are carried to the **liver, spleen, bone marrow and gall bladder**, where they multiply further in the reticuloendothelial system.
6. Release from these sites gives the **secondary, persistent bacteraemia**, which marks the **onset of clinical illness** — **step-ladder fever**, headache, malaise, relative bradycardia, rose spots, splenomegaly and constipation (or diarrhoea in children).
7. **Endotoxin (lipid A)** released from the bacilli causes the fever, toxaemia and myocardial depression.
8. From the gall bladder, bacilli are **excreted in bile back into the intestine**, re-invading the **already sensitised Peyer's patches**, which undergo **hyperplasia → necrosis → ulceration** in the third week.
9. These longitudinal ulcers may cause the classical complications — **intestinal haemorrhage and perforation** (third week), peritonitis.
10. Some patients continue to harbour the organism in the gall bladder beyond one year — the **chronic carrier state**, especially with gallstones.

## 695
**Answer**

**Role of the Widal test in enteric fever**

The Widal test is a **tube agglutination test** that detects **antibodies in the patient's serum** against the **O (somatic)** and **H (flagellar)** antigens of *Salmonella* Typhi and Paratyphi A and B.

**Value**
1. It is **cheap, simple and widely available**, which keeps it in use in endemic areas with limited facilities.
2. It becomes positive from the **end of the first week**, when blood cultures begin to fall off, so it supports a **presumptive diagnosis in the second week**.
3. A **fourfold rise in titre between paired sera** 7–10 days apart is good evidence of recent infection.
4. **O antibody** (early, falls in months) suggests **recent infection**; **H antibody** (late, persists for years) is less useful.
5. **Anti-Vi antibody** helps detect the **chronic carrier**.

1. **It is not confirmatory** — isolation of the organism is the only definitive diagnosis.
2. **Negative in the first week**, and in patients given early antibiotics.
3. **False positives** from past infection, prior **TAB vaccination**, anamnestic response in unrelated fevers, and cross-reaction with other *Salmonella*, malaria and other enterobacteria.
4. **False negatives** in carriers, in early treatment, and with poor antibody response.
5. A **single titre is hard to interpret in endemic areas**, where the baseline titre of the healthy population is already raised — hence local baseline titres must be known.

## 702
**Answer**

**Pathogenesis of cholera**
1. A **large infecting dose (10⁸–10¹⁰)** is needed, since the organism is **highly acid-sensitive**; achlorhydria, antacids and food buffering lower this.
2. Organisms reach the **small intestine**, and penetrate the mucous layer using **motility (polar flagellum), mucinase and neuraminidase**.
3. They **adhere to the epithelium by the toxin co-regulated pilus (TCP)** and other adhesins — but are **entirely non-invasive**; the mucosa remains histologically intact, which is why there is no blood, pus or fever.
4. They elaborate **cholera toxin (choleragen)**, a classic **A–B₅ enterotoxin** encoded by the lysogenic **CTXφ phage**.
5. **Five B subunits bind the GM1 ganglioside receptor** on the enterocyte; the **A subunit** enters and its **A1 fragment ADP-ribosylates the Gsα regulatory protein**.
6. This **locks adenylate cyclase in the "on" state**, causing a sustained rise in **intracellular cyclic AMP**.
7. Raised cAMP activates protein kinase A, which **phosphorylates the CFTR channel**, producing:
   - **Massive active secretion of chloride and bicarbonate** from the crypt cells
   - **Blockade of coupled sodium–chloride absorption** by villous cells
8. **Water follows osmotically** — in volumes up to **1 litre per hour**, far beyond colonic absorptive capacity.
9. The result is **profuse painless watery diarrhoea with "rice-watery" stools** containing mucus flecks and desquamated cells, leading to **severe dehydration, hypovolaemic shock, metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate loss), hypokalaemia, muscle cramps and acute renal failure**.
10. Death is from **dehydration and electrolyte loss**, not from invasion — hence **prompt rehydration is life-saving** and reduces mortality from over 50% to under 1%.

## 704
**Answer**

**Mechanism of action of cholera toxin in producing acute watery diarrhoea**
1. **Cholera toxin (choleragen)** is a **heat-labile A–B₅ enterotoxin** of 84 kDa, encoded by the **CTXφ bacteriophage**; it consists of **one A subunit and five B subunits**.
2. The **five B (binding) subunits bind irreversibly to the GM1 ganglioside receptor** on the surface of the small intestinal enterocyte.
3. The **A subunit is internalised** and cleaved into **A1 (active)** and A2 fragments; A1 enters the cytosol.
4. **A1 catalyses the transfer of ADP-ribose from NAD⁺ onto the Gsα subunit** of the stimulatory G protein — **ADP-ribosylation**.
5. This **inhibits the intrinsic GTPase activity** of Gsα, so it remains bound to GTP and **permanently activates adenylate cyclase**.
6. **Intracellular cyclic AMP rises** persistently (up to a hundredfold), activating **protein kinase A**.
7. Protein kinase A **phosphorylates the CFTR chloride channel**, with two consequences:
   - **Crypt cells actively secrete Cl⁻, Na⁺, K⁺ and HCO₃⁻** into the lumen
   - **Villous cells stop absorbing coupled NaCl**
8. The osmotic gradient draws **water into the lumen** in enormous volumes (up to 1 L/hour).
9. Since the mucosa is **not invaded or damaged**, the stool is **painless, afebrile, non-inflammatory "rice-water"**, with no blood or pus.
10. The massive loss of **water, sodium, potassium and bicarbonate** causes **dehydration, hypovolaemic shock, metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia and renal failure**.
11. The effect lasts the **lifespan of the enterocyte (a few days)**, because the modification is irreversible — hence **rehydration, not antitoxin, is the treatment**.

   - **Mucinase, protease, phospholipase** degrading the protective mucus layer
6. **Inflammation:** lipopolysaccharide and the above factors attract **neutrophils and lymphocytes**, with release of **IL-8, TNF-α and IL-1β**, producing **chronic active gastritis**.
7. **Altered acid physiology:** antral infection reduces **somatostatin from D cells**, which disinhibits **gastrin from G cells**, causing **hypergastrinaemia and increased acid output** from the intact corpus — this acid load strikes the **duodenum**, causing **gastric metaplasia** there, which *H. pylori* then colonises → **duodenal ulcer**.
8. In corpus-predominant infection, the gland atrophy leads to **hypochlorhydria, gastric ulcer** and ultimately **gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma** — *H. pylori* is a **WHO group I carcinogen**.
9. The net result is a breach of mucosal defence with persisting acid–pepsin attack, producing the ulcer.

**Laboratory diagnosis of *H. pylori***

**A. Invasive (needs endoscopic biopsy from the antrum)**
1. **Rapid urease test (RUT/CLO test)** — biopsy in urea–phenol red medium; a **pink colour within minutes to hours** indicates urease. Quick and cheap.
2. **Histopathology** — Giemsa, Warthin–Starry silver, or H&E; shows the curved bacilli with gastritis. Considered a reference standard.
3. **Culture** — on **chocolate, Skirrow's or brain–heart infusion agar with blood**, **microaerophilic (5% O₂, 10% CO₂)**, 37 °C for 3–7 days; small translucent colonies. **Oxidase, catalase and urease positive**. Slow and insensitive, but gives **antibiotic sensitivity**.
4. **PCR** on biopsy — also detects clarithromycin resistance mutations.

**B. Non-invasive**
1. **Urea breath test (¹³C or ¹⁴C)** — the **best non-invasive test**, for diagnosis and for **confirming eradication**; labelled CO₂ appears in the breath.
2. **Stool antigen test (ELISA)** — good for diagnosis and follow-up, especially in children.
3. **Serology (ELISA for IgG)** — indicates exposure, but **remains positive after eradication**, so it is unsuitable for confirming cure.

*(PPIs, antibiotics and bismuth should be stopped 2–4 weeks before testing to avoid false negatives.)*

**Short note: *Helicobacter pylori***

- **Morphology:** Gram-negative, **curved, spiral or S-shaped bacillus**, 3 µm long, with **4–6 sheathed polar flagella** giving rapid corkscrew motility; **microaerophilic**.
- **Habitat:** the **mucus layer over the gastric antral epithelium** in humans; acquired in childhood, faeco-oral or oro-oral.
- **Key enzyme:** **strongly urease-positive** — the basis of the rapid urease test and the urea breath test; also **oxidase and catalase positive**.
- **Virulence factors:** urease (ammonia cloud neutralising acid), flagella and spiral shape (penetration of mucus), **BabA adhesin**, **VacA (vacuolating cytotoxin)**, **CagA with type IV secretion system**, mucinase, protease, LPS.
- **Diseases:** **chronic active (type B) antral gastritis**, **duodenal ulcer (over 90%)**, **gastric ulcer (70–80%)**, **gastric adenocarcinoma** and **gastric MALT lymphoma** — classified by IARC as a **group I carcinogen**.
- **Diagnosis**
  - **Invasive:** rapid urease (CLO) test, histology (Giemsa, Warthin–Starry), culture on Skirrow's medium microaerophilically for 3–7 days, PCR
  - **Non-invasive:** **urea breath test** (best for confirming eradication), **stool antigen test**, serology (IgG ELISA — cannot confirm cure)
- **Treatment:** **triple therapy** — a **PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin (or metronidazole)** for 14 days; **quadruple therapy with bismuth** where clarithromycin resistance is high. Eradication is confirmed by urea breath or stool antigen test 4 weeks after therapy.

|---|---|---|

**Atypical characteristics of *Chlamydia***

*Chlamydia* are **obligate intracellular bacteria**, once mistaken for viruses, and they share features of both:

**Bacterial features**
1. Contain **both DNA and RNA**
2. Possess a **cell wall resembling that of Gram-negative bacteria** (though it **lacks muramic acid in the peptidoglycan**)
3. Possess **ribosomes (70S)** and synthesise their own proteins and nucleic acids
4. **Multiply by binary fission**
5. **Susceptible to antibiotics** — tetracyclines, macrolides

**Virus-like (atypical) features**
1. **Obligate intracellular parasites** — they cannot be grown on cell-free (inanimate) media, and need cell culture, yolk sac or laboratory animals
2. **"Energy parasites"** — they lack the cytochrome system and **cannot generate their own ATP**, depending on the host cell
3. Very **small size** (0.3 µm elementary body), passing some bacterial filters
4. A **unique biphasic developmental cycle**:
   - **Elementary body (EB)** — small, rigid, metabolically inert, **extracellular and infective**
   - **Reticulate body (RB)** — large, fragile, metabolically active, **intracellular and non-infective, the replicating form**
5. Form characteristic **intracytoplasmic inclusions** (**glycogen-containing, iodine-staining in *C. trachomatis***)
6. **Lack muramic acid**, so they are **not susceptible to penicillin** or other cell-wall-active drugs in the usual way
