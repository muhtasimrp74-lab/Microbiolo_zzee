## 592
**Answer**

**Drug resistance** is the ability of a microorganism to **survive and multiply in the presence of a concentration of an antimicrobial agent that normally inhibits or kills organisms of that species**.

- In laboratory terms, the **MIC** of the drug for the strain is **above the concentration achievable in blood or tissue** at the usual dose.
- **Types:**
  - **Natural (intrinsic):** an inherent property of the species, e.g. Gram-negative bacilli to penicillin G and vancomycin, *Mycoplasma* to β-lactams (no cell wall), anaerobes to aminoglycosides.
  - **Acquired:** a previously sensitive organism becomes resistant, by **mutation** or **gene transfer**.
- **Multidrug resistance (MDR):** resistance to three or more classes of antimicrobial.

## 593
**Answer**

**Mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance in bacteria**

**1. Enzymatic inactivation or modification of the drug** (the commonest)
- **β-lactamases**, including **ESBLs**, AmpC and **carbapenemases (NDM-1, KPC, OXA-48)**.
- **Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes** (acetyl-, adenyl- and phospho-transferases).
- **Chloramphenicol acetyltransferase**, and erythromycin esterase.

**2. Alteration of the drug target**
- **Altered PBP:** **PBP2a (*mecA*)** in **MRSA**, and altered PBPs in penicillin-resistant pneumococci.
- **Ribosomal methylation:** *erm* gene (macrolides, MLSB resistance), and 30S changes (streptomycin).
- **Altered DNA gyrase** (quinolones), **altered RNA polymerase** (rifampicin), and altered **D-ala-D-lac** peptidoglycan precursor (*vanA*, VRE).
- Altered dihydropteroate synthase and dihydrofolate reductase (sulphonamides, trimethoprim).

**3. Decreased permeability (reduced uptake)**
- **Loss or alteration of porins** in Gram-negatives (imipenem resistance in *Pseudomonas*, OprD loss).
- The impermeable mycobacterial wall.

**4. Active efflux pumps**
- Tetracyclines (*tet*), macrolides (*mef*), fluoroquinolones, and the multidrug efflux pumps of *P. aeruginosa* and *Acinetobacter*.

**5. Bypass or overproduction of the target**
- Alternative, drug-resistant **dihydrofolate reductase** (trimethoprim), and overproduction of PABA (sulphonamides).

**6. Other mechanisms**
- **Biofilm formation** (catheters, prostheses), protecting embedded cells.
- **Persisters and dormancy** (phenotypic tolerance), and the L-form state.
- Failure of **prodrug activation**: loss of catalase-peroxidase (*katG*) causes isoniazid resistance; reduced nitroreductase causes metronidazole resistance.

## 594
**Answer**

**Genetic basis of resistance**

**A. Chromosomal (mutational)**
- Spontaneous, random **mutation** of a chromosomal gene, occurring at a low rate (about 1 in 10^7–10^9).
- The antibiotic does not cause the mutation; it **selects** the resistant mutant (selection pressure).
- Usually resistance to **one drug**, and often with reduced fitness.
- May be **single-step and high-level** (streptomycin, rifampicin) or **multi-step and stepwise** (penicillin in pneumococci).
- Avoided clinically by **combination therapy**, as in tuberculosis.

**B. Extrachromosomal (transferable)** — the more important clinically
- **Plasmid-mediated (R factors)**, often carrying **multidrug resistance**, and spread by:
  1. **Conjugation** — the main route, through the sex pilus, across species and genera;
  2. **Transduction** — by bacteriophage, e.g. penicillinase in staphylococci;
  3. **Transformation** — uptake of free DNA, e.g. in pneumococci and *Neisseria* (mosaic PBP genes).
- **Transposons and integrons** move resistance cassettes between plasmid, chromosome and phage.

**Non-genetic basis of resistance (phenotypic tolerance)**
1. **Metabolically inactive or dormant organisms** ("persisters"): β-lactams need growing cells, so resting organisms survive, as in tuberculous and other chronic foci.
2. **Loss of the target structure:** **L-forms and protoplasts**, which have no cell wall, so β-lactams are ineffective; they revert later and cause relapse.
3. **Intracellular location** of the organism, where the drug penetrates poorly (*Salmonella* and *M. tuberculosis* inside macrophages).
4. **Sequestered or avascular sites**: abscess cavity, vegetations, empyema, prostate and CSF, and foreign bodies, where the drug concentration is inadequate.
5. **Local conditions:** acid pH and anaerobiosis in pus reduce aminoglycoside activity; **pus and tissue debris contain PABA**, antagonising sulphonamides.
6. **Biofilms** on catheters and prostheses.

## 595
**Answer**

**How a drug-resistance gene is transferred between bacterial cells.** By **horizontal gene transfer**, chiefly through plasmids carrying resistance (R) genes.

1. **Conjugation** (the most important)
   - Requires **direct cell contact** through a **sex (F) pilus** encoded by *tra* genes of a conjugative plasmid.
   - The **R factor** has two components: the **RTF** (resistance transfer factor, which carries the transfer genes) and the **r-determinant** (the resistance genes).
   - One strand of the plasmid is transferred and each cell then synthesises the complementary strand, so **both cells become donors**.
   - Crosses **species and genus barriers** (e.g. *E. coli* → *Klebsiella* → *Salmonella*). It also occurs between Gram-positives, by pheromone-responsive plasmids and conjugative transposons (VRE → MRSA).
   - It is the main route of **epidemic multidrug resistance** (ESBL, NDM-1).

2. **Transduction**
   - Transfer of resistance DNA by a **bacteriophage**, e.g. **penicillinase in *S. aureus***. Limited to organisms sharing a common phage.

3. **Transformation**
   - Uptake of **free (naked) DNA** released by lysed resistant bacteria, by a **competent** cell, e.g. **mosaic PBP genes** in penicillin-resistant *S. pneumoniae* and *N. gonorrhoeae*.

4. **Transposition**
   - **Transposons** ("jumping genes") and **integrons** move resistance cassettes between plasmid, chromosome and phage, assembling multidrug-resistance regions.

**Mechanisms of genetic transfer between bacteria:** these same four processes — transformation, transduction, conjugation and transposition — as described in **Q527**.

## 596
**Answer**

**Role of plasmids in drug resistance** — see **Q505**.

In brief: **R plasmids** carry genes for drug-inactivating enzymes (β-lactamase, ESBL, carbapenemase, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes), altered targets, efflux pumps and bypass enzymes; one plasmid may carry **resistance to several unrelated drugs**; and they spread **rapidly by conjugation across species**, driven by antibiotic selection pressure, which is the main cause of epidemic multidrug resistance.

## 597
**Answer**

**How bacteria develop resistance to penicillin**
1. **Production of β-lactamase (penicillinase)** — the commonest mechanism.
   - The enzyme **hydrolyses the β-lactam ring**, inactivating the drug.
   - In *S. aureus* it is **plasmid-encoded** and **inducible**, and spread by **transduction**. Over 90% of hospital strains are penicillinase producers.
   - In Gram-negatives the enzymes are periplasmic and often plasmid-borne, and include **ESBLs** and **carbapenemases**.
   - Countered by **β-lactamase-stable penicillins** (cloxacillin, methicillin) and **β-lactamase inhibitors** (clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam).
2. **Alteration of the target (penicillin-binding proteins)**
   - **MRSA** acquires the ***mecA* gene** (on SCC*mec*), producing **PBP2a**, which has a very low affinity for all β-lactams. This gives resistance to every penicillin and cephalosporin.
   - **Penicillin-resistant pneumococci and gonococci** acquire **mosaic PBP genes** by **transformation**.
3. **Decreased permeability:** loss or alteration of **porins** in the Gram-negative outer membrane, so the drug cannot reach the PBPs.
4. **Active efflux** of the drug from the periplasm.
5. **Tolerance:** the organism is inhibited but not killed, because **autolysins fail to be activated**.
6. **Non-genetic:** L-forms, which have no cell wall and so no target.

**Indications for chemoprophylaxis** (antimicrobials given to a person who is not infected, to prevent infection)

**1. Medical (prevention of a specific infection after exposure or in a susceptible person)**
- **Rheumatic fever:** long-term benzathine penicillin to prevent recurrence.
- **Meningococcal meningitis:** rifampicin or ciprofloxacin for close contacts.
- **Tuberculosis:** isoniazid for contacts, for children and for HIV-positive people (latent TB).
- Malaria prophylaxis for travellers; **post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV** and for diphtheria, pertussis and plague contacts.
- **Recurrent UTI**, and prophylaxis in **asplenic** and immunocompromised patients.
- *Pneumocystis jirovecii* prophylaxis with co-trimoxazole when the CD4 count is below 200.

**2. Surgical (prevention of surgical-site infection)**
- **Clean-contaminated and contaminated surgery:** gastrointestinal, biliary, colorectal, gynaecological and urological operations.
- Clean surgery with an **implant or prosthesis** (joint replacement, heart valve, mesh, shunt).
- Cardiac, vascular and neurosurgical procedures; **caesarean section**, amputation and compound fractures.
- Given as a single dose intravenously **30–60 minutes before incision** so that tissue levels are adequate at the time of incision.

**3. Prevention of infective endocarditis** in high-risk cardiac patients (prosthetic valve, previous endocarditis, certain congenital disease) before dental and some other procedures.

**Principles:** use for a **specific organism**, for a **short, defined period**, with an **effective and narrow-spectrum drug**. Prolonged or indiscriminate prophylaxis selects resistance and causes superinfection.

## 599
**Answer**

**Short note: Transduction**
- **Definition:** the transfer of a portion of DNA from a **donor to a recipient bacterium by a bacteriophage** acting as the vector.
- **Discovered by** Zinder and Lederberg (1952) in *Salmonella* Typhimurium, with phage P22.

**Types**
1. **Generalised transduction**
   - Occurs in the **lytic cycle**. During assembly, a fragment of **host DNA is packaged by mistake** into the phage head.
   - **Any gene** may be transferred, at random.
   - *Examples:* phage P22 in *Salmonella*, P1 in *E. coli*, and transfer of **penicillinase resistance in *S. aureus***.
2. **Specialised (restricted) transduction**
   - Occurs in the **lysogenic cycle**. The **prophage excises imprecisely**, carrying adjacent host genes with it.
   - Only **genes adjacent to the prophage insertion site** are transferred.
   - *Example:* phage **lambda** in *E. coli*, transferring the **gal and bio** genes.
3. **Abortive transduction:** the transferred DNA is not integrated and is passed to only one daughter cell at each division.

**Related process — lysogenic (phage) conversion:** here the **phage's own genes**, not host genes, give the bacterium a new property, e.g. **diphtheria toxin**, botulinum toxins C and D, erythrogenic toxin, Shiga toxin and cholera toxin.

**Importance**
1. Spread of **drug resistance** (staphylococcal penicillinase) and of virulence genes.
2. A tool in **genetic mapping** and recombinant DNA technology.
3. **Phage typing** in epidemiology.
