## 499
**Answer**

**Sporulation** is the process by which a vegetative bacterial cell forms an **endospore**.

**When it occurs:** under **adverse conditions**, chiefly **nutrient depletion** (carbon, nitrogen, phosphate), and also desiccation, accumulation of toxic metabolites and unfavourable temperature. It takes about 6–8 hours.

**How it occurs (stages)**
1. **Axial filament formation:** DNA replicates and forms an axial chromatin thread.
2. **Asymmetric septum formation:** the cell membrane invaginates, giving a small **forespore** and a larger mother cell.
3. **Engulfment:** the mother cell engulfs the forespore, which now has two membranes.
4. **Cortex formation:** peptidoglycan is laid down between the membranes; **calcium dipicolinate** accumulates and the core dehydrates.
5. **Coat formation:** keratin-like protein coats and the exosporium are deposited.
6. **Maturation and lysis:** the spore matures, the mother cell lyses and the **free spore** is liberated.

Sporulation is controlled by **sigma factors** (σF, σE, σG, σK) and the Spo0A regulator.

**Germination** (the reverse) occurs in favourable conditions, in three steps: **activation** (heat or abrasion), **initiation** (loss of refractility, release of dipicolinic acid) and **outgrowth** into a vegetative cell.

## 500
**Answer**

**Why endospores are highly resistant to heat and chemicals**
1. **Dehydration of the core:** the core contains only 10–30% water, so proteins are immobilised and resist heat denaturation. This is the main reason.
2. **Calcium dipicolinate (CaDPA)**, 5–15% of the spore's dry weight. It replaces water, stabilises DNA and proteins and confers heat resistance.
3. **Small acid-soluble proteins (SASPs)** saturate the DNA, protecting it from heat, UV radiation, desiccation and chemicals.
4. **Thick cortex** of loosely cross-linked peptidoglycan, which maintains core dehydration by osmotic contraction.
5. **Impermeable keratin-like spore coat and exosporium**, which exclude chemicals, dyes, enzymes and disinfectants.
6. **Metabolic dormancy:** no metabolism means no target for antibiotics and no enzyme activity to be destroyed.
7. **Efficient DNA repair enzymes** on germination.

**Consequence:** spores survive boiling. Reliable killing requires an **autoclave (121 °C, 15 psi, 15 min)**, hot air oven (160 °C for 2 h), ethylene oxide, glutaraldehyde or gamma irradiation.

## 509
**Answer**

**Sequence of events in the bacterial growth cycle.** When bacteria are inoculated into a fresh liquid medium and counted at intervals, the growth curve shows four phases.

1. **Lag phase** (a few minutes to hours)
   - No increase in cell number, but the cells are **metabolically very active**: enzymes and ribosomes are synthesised, and the cells enlarge.
   - They adapt to the new environment.
2. **Log (exponential) phase**
   - Cells divide at a **constant, maximal rate** (constant generation time); a straight line on a log plot.
   - The cells are uniform, of maximum size, and **most susceptible to antibiotics** and adverse agents. They stain typically.
3. **Stationary phase**
   - Nutrient depletion and accumulation of toxic products mean that **the rate of multiplication equals the rate of death**, so the viable count is constant.
   - **Spores, exotoxins, antibiotics and secondary metabolites** are produced; cells are pleomorphic and store granules.
4. **Decline (death) phase**
   - Death exceeds multiplication, so the viable count falls **exponentially**.
   - **Involution forms** appear, with autolysis.

*(A phase of prolonged decline or survival may follow.)*

## 510
**Answer**

**Bacterial growth** is an **orderly increase in all the chemical components of the cell, leading to an increase in cell size and then to cell division**. In practice, growth of a bacterial culture means **an increase in the number of cells**, which multiply by **binary fission** in geometric progression (1 → 2 → 4 → 8 …).
- The time taken for one cell to divide into two, or for the population to double, is the **generation (doubling) time**, about **20 min for *E. coli*** and **18–24 h for *M. tuberculosis***.

**Death of bacteria** means the **irreversible loss of the ability to multiply (reproduce)**, even when transferred to a favourable fresh medium. A cell may still appear intact and metabolise, yet be "dead" in this sense.
- Death is **exponential** at a constant rate, so it is expressed as **D value** (decimal reduction time, the time to kill 90% of the population).
- Caused by heat, radiation, chemicals, antibiotics or starvation; and bacteria may autolyse.

**Answer**

**Why some bacteria are anaerobic.** They are anaerobic because they **lack the enzymes that detoxify the toxic by-products of oxygen metabolism** and because their metabolism does not require oxygen.

## 518
**Answer**

**Why anaerobic bacteria cannot grow in the presence of oxygen**
1. During aerobic metabolism, oxygen is reduced to highly toxic intermediates:
   - **superoxide anion (O2•−)**;
   - **hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)**;
   - **hydroxyl free radical (OH•)**, the most damaging.
2. Aerobes and facultative anaerobes possess **superoxide dismutase** (converting superoxide to H2O2 and O2) and **catalase or peroxidase** (destroying H2O2). Anaerobes **lack these enzymes**.
3. So in the presence of oxygen these radicals accumulate and cause **lipid peroxidation of the membrane, protein oxidation and DNA strand breaks**, killing the cell.
4. Their **essential enzymes contain –SH groups** that are irreversibly oxidised by oxygen (e.g. pyruvate-ferredoxin oxidoreductase).
5. They need a **low redox potential (Eh, about −200 mV)**. Oxygen raises the Eh and inhibits growth.

**Practical consequence:** specimens for anaerobic culture must be transported in an **oxygen-free medium**, and cultured in an anaerobic jar, GasPak or Robertson's cooked meat medium. Anaerobic infections occur where tissue Eh is lowered, as in deep wounds with necrosis or poor blood supply.

- **Mesophiles** are bacteria that grow best at **moderate temperatures**, with an optimum of about **25–40 °C** (usually 37 °C). Their range is roughly 10–45 °C.
- **Almost all bacteria pathogenic for humans are mesophiles**, since 37 °C is body temperature.
- *Examples:* *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *Salmonella* Typhi, *M. tuberculosis*, *S. pneumoniae*.
- **Importance:**
  1. They grow in the body and in routine laboratory incubation at **37 °C**.
  2. They multiply in **food kept at room temperature**, which is the basis of food poisoning, so food is refrigerated or kept hot.
  3. Growth temperature is used in **identification**, e.g. *Listeria* (a psychrotroph) grows at 4 °C with cold enrichment, and *Campylobacter* at 42 °C.
- **Compared with:** **psychrophiles** (optimum below 20 °C, e.g. *Pseudomonas*, *Listeria*) and **thermophiles** (55–80 °C, e.g. *Bacillus stearothermophilus*, used as a sterilisation control).

**How bacteria transmit genetic material.** Besides vertical transmission to daughter cells, bacteria exchange genes **horizontally** by four mechanisms.

1. **Transformation**
   - Uptake of **naked (free) DNA** from the surrounding medium by a **competent** recipient cell.
   - Discovered by **Griffith (1928)** with pneumococci, and shown to involve DNA by Avery, MacLeod and McCarty.
   - *Examples:* *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *Neisseria*, *Bacillus*.
2. **Transduction**
   - Transfer of DNA from donor to recipient **by a bacteriophage**.
   - **Generalised:** any fragment of host DNA is packaged at random (lytic cycle), e.g. in *Salmonella* (phage P22) and *S. aureus*.
   - **Specialised (restricted):** only genes adjacent to the prophage site are transferred, e.g. the *gal* and *bio* genes by phage lambda in *E. coli*.
   - *(Lysogenic conversion is related: the phage's own genes give toxin production, e.g. diphtheria, botulinum C/D, Shiga and cholera toxins.)*
3. **Conjugation**
   - Transfer of DNA by **direct cell-to-cell contact** through the **sex (F) pilus**, the F+ donor to the F− recipient. An **Hfr** cell transfers chromosomal genes.
   - The main route of spread of **R (drug-resistance) plasmids**, even across species.
4. **Transposition**
   - Movement of **transposons ("jumping genes")** and **integrons** within or between DNA molecules (chromosome, plasmid, phage). They often carry resistance genes.

**Importance:** these mechanisms produce **genetic variation**, spread **multidrug resistance** and virulence, and are used in genetic mapping and recombinant DNA technology.
